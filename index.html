A Validation-Driven Bilevel Optimization Method for Mesh-Free PDE Solvers and Its Error Analysis

Yongcun Song, Shangzhi Zeng, Jin Zhang, Lvgang Zhang

Mesh-free partial differential equation (PDE) solvers construct discrete systems from residuals at sampled points. These solvers fit coefficients in a parameterized trial representation and select solver-design parameters governing the representation, training-residual weighting, and regularization. These parameters strongly affect solution accuracy, yet existing selection strategies are largely tailored to particular solver classes. To address this limitation, we formulate a unified validation-driven bilevel optimization framework applicable across different solver classes, in which the lower level fits coefficients using training collocation residuals, while the upper level selects solver-design parameters using held-out validation residuals. For nonlinear PDEs, however, solving the nonlinear lower-level problem to high accuracy is costly, while the adjoint system is typically ill-conditioned and indefinite. To address this issue, we propose an efficient implicit hypergradient method that approximates the lower-level response using finite, warm-started damped Gauss-Newton (GN) updates. For the adjoint solve, we use the damped GN matrix to compute an initial adjoint and to precondition a finite number of minimum residual iterations. To quantify the errors introduced by these approximations, we derive local hypergradient error bounds that separate the contributions of the finite lower-level solve, adjoint damping, and the final adjoint residual. Combining this analysis with validation sampling bounds and residual stability, we relate validation residuals to PDE solution accuracy and establish error bounds for the computed PDE solution. Numerical experiments on several nonlinear PDEs, including a high-dimensional problem, demonstrate the accuracy and efficiency of the proposed method across Kansa, random feature and extreme learning machine, and physics-informed neural network solvers.

Atomic MathReasoner: Instance-Level Composition of Atomic Reasoning Skills for Mathematical LLMs

Jiashu Li, Tianyu Gao, Qinglong Liu, Jinping Wang, Ye Wei, Zhiqiang Gao

Most existing approaches enhance mathematical reasoning in large language models (LLMs) as a monolithic capability, despite the fact that different mathematical problems require different reasoning skills. Recent work has explored a more fine-grained perspective by decomposing mathematical reasoning into atomic skills and developing specialized modules for individual capabilities. However, how to effectively utilize these atomic abilities remains underexplored. To address this challenge, we propose Atomic MathReasoner (AMR), an instance-level framework for composing atomic reasoning skills. AMR first trains a library of capability-specific LoRA experts and performs gradient-free optimization over held-out validation subsets to discover a diverse set of low-loss composition prototypes. It evaluates the preference of each prototype using the token-level negative log-likelihood of reference solutions, filters ambiguous assignments through a confidence margin, and distills the resulting pseudo-labels into a lightweight router. During inference, the router selects an appropriate composition prototype for each problem, enabling problem-specific utilization of atomic skills without costly online optimization. Experiments on the AtomicMath benchmark across general and mathematics-specialized LLMs demonstrate consistent improvements. On Qwen2.5-Math-Instruct-7B, AMR increases average field accuracy from 64.4% to 68.1% at Level 1 and from 59.3% to 63.7% at Level 2, while improving average logical reasoning performance from 39.4% to 46.8%. These results demonstrate that effectively composing specialized atomic skills provides a promising approach to strengthening mathematical reasoning in LLMs.

Late-Time Dynamics of Flow Matching: Distance Geometry and Learning

Umberto Biccari, Junqing Huang, Kang Liu, Enrique Zuazua

Generative models transform simple random inputs into samples that resemble observed data. For a finite training set, a central question is how its geometry shapes the terminal dynamics and the probabilities assigned to individual samples. We address this question for flow matching with linear interpolation between independent Gaussian and empirical samples. The squared distance to the training set generates explicit paths contracting exponentially toward the nearest sample. We prove that exact trajectories restarted at prescribed late-time states approximate these paths uniformly over all elapsed times and compact sets of starting states away from Voronoi interfaces, with an error exponentially small in the inverse squared restart noise scale. The initial mass near interfaces controls the quadratic Wasserstein error between the exact and reference laws. For Gaussian and fixed Gaussian-tube restart laws, this gives a square-root rate in the restart noise scale, uniform over time, which a two-site example shows to be sharp in general. At ties, the Clarke subdifferential characterizes the graphical limit of the rescaled velocity field. This geometry motivates two learning methods: fitting the distance potential with value and gradient supervision, and fitting velocities along explicit contraction paths. A transport estimate separates initialization mismatch, velocity error, incomplete contraction, and endpoint allocation. MNIST experiments illustrate the distinct roles of support proximity, nearest-site retention, and feature fidelity. These results connect flow matching and score-based diffusion through a common limiting geometry and show how asymptotic analysis can inform the design and evaluation of generative models.

The Four-Element Theory of Structure-Driven Methodology: A Methodological Shift from the Perspective of Natural Science and Engineering Practice

Shengchang Chen

Mainstream artificial intelligence systems, fundamentally driven by the Optimization-Driven (OD) paradigm, rely on setting external objectives and iterative trial-and-error. When confronted with complex nonlinear problems containing noise and conflicting constraints, this paradigm inherently induces statistical smoothing and compromise, yielding black-box models that lack interpretability and out-of-distribution generalization. To address these limitations, this paper proposes the Four-Element Theory of Structure-Driven Methodology, reconstructing the solving process into four irreversible stages: Deconstruction, Structure, Construction, and Solving. The method suspends a priori presuppositions to strip unit coupling, extracts rigid invariants inherent to the problem, constructs equivalent mappings anchored to these invariants, and executes the mapping directly to generate the solution. Evaluation metrics and experience are relegated to a posteriori verification. Demonstrated through cross-disciplinary practices including number theory, Hamiltonian systems, deep learning, and seismic inversion, this methodology transcends the statistical fitting bottleneck. Structure-Driven and Objective-Driven methodologies are complementary: when structure is sufficient, solutions are directly constructed; when lacking, it degenerates into the OD path.

Hai Wang, Bretislav Hajek, Chonghe Jiang, Minwei Kong, Ao Qu, Huatian Gong

Large language model (LLM) agents now write, test and improve code, but whether they can design algorithms that scale to realistic instances is open: in the FrontierOR benchmark's one-shot evaluation, the strongest model matches Gurobi, with an objective within 1% of Gurobi's and no slower, on only 39% of the large instances. The FrontierOR Challenge asks teams to build agentic workflows that design, test, and improve algorithms for many optimization problems at once. Stage 1 releases 60 of the benchmark's 180 audited problems, spanning six application categories and five formulation types, in weekly rounds of 15; Stage 2 introduces new problems outside the benchmark, with unseen instances. Two tracks are ranked separately: in the Main track we run the submitted algorithms in a network-isolated sandbox with open-source dependencies only; in the Self-hosted track teams solve the instances on their own machines, with any software, within a timed window. Every solution is checked for feasibility, and a feasible solution scores the ratio of its objective to a frozen known reference, averaged over hidden instances.

Michael G. Medvedev, Nikolai V. Krivoshchapov, Stepan N. Ostarkov

Language models are now used throughout all sciences - for reading literature, extracting numbers from tables and spectra, analyzing data, drafting conclusions - and their answers arrive in the same polished confident tone whether they are right or wrong, so a result has to be re-derived by a human researcher before it can be relied on. The cost of that re-checking, not the ability to generate results, is what currently limits AI-assisted research. In the field of mathematics, this problem is solved by proof assistants like Lean 4 and Coq that let any formal statement and its proof be written down and checked - a perfect match for LLMs, which are now actively used to search for proofs with every step automatically validated, rather than relying on a human to judge the result. In this paper, we propose an extension of this approach to non-mathematical sciences. We describe an AI agent harness that aims to make LLM's scientific reasoning verifiable: the starting assumptions are proposed by LLM and reviewed by a human, and everything built on top of them is checked by a machine with Lean 4. We illustrate how this mechanism works in practice on several examples and our results show that scientific arguments in these tasks can be expressed as formal proofs from explicit assumptions, including experimental results, known theoretical statements, and additional assumptions, produced by the agent. We suggest that this approach could change how scientific research is conducted across all sciences.

AI-Assisted Decision Support for Formula 1 Pit-Stop Strategy: Deep Reinforcement Learning and Mathematical Programming

Felipe Méndez, Charles Thraves

Pit-stop strategy in Formula 1 is a sequential decision problem under uncertainty, in which strategists decide at each lap whether to pit and which tire compound to fit. We develop a data-driven simulator, calibrated with historical race data, that captures tire degradation, drivers' recent performance, overtaking, yellow flag events, and rivals' pit-stop decisions. We train agents with five Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithms and compare them with a Quadratic Mixed-Integer Programming (QMIP) formulation that is optimal in deterministic single-driver settings. Without uncertainty or competition, all DRL methods except TRPO achieve near-optimal performance. With a full grid of competitors and stochastic race events, the ranking of DRL methods depends on the evaluation setting: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its recurrent variant are the only methods that improve on the QMIP both in fully simulated races and when the actual 2024 races are replayed. Averaged over eight circuits, PPO improves on the QMIP by 0.33 and 0.49 positions in the two settings and Recurrent PPO by less, and in the replay PPO finishes 0.66 positions ahead of the drivers' actual strategies simulated in the same races. The QMIP performs similarly to the other methods, even though it cannot anticipate yellow flag events. Finally, we use the trained PPO policy as a look-ahead decision-support tool that shows the strategist the distribution of race outcomes under each candidate pit-stop action. Applied retrospectively to eight 2024 races, the look-ahead identifies the laps where the actual decision, the recommendation, or a third action was preferable.

PWLNet: A Framework for Linearized Neural Surrogates in Nonlinear Process Optimization

Sunoy Sanyal, Canberk Demir, Metin Türkay, Erdal Aydın

Neural networks represent nonlinear process systems accurately, but embedding them in optimization is difficult because smooth activations introduce nonlinear, nonconvex constraints. PWLNet is a Python framework for building optimization-compatible neural surrogates through exact and piecewise-linear reformulations. It combines baseline and physics-informed training, optimization-aware pruning, and Pyomo embeddings based on ReLU mixed-integer linear models, exact tanh nonlinear models, PWL-tanh formulations, and binary-free SOSX branch-and-LP. PWL-native training uses the same activation in training and embedding, avoiding extra linearization error. It is evaluated on Boston housing, the Williams-Otto process, the Van de Vusse reactor, and a hydroformylation semi-batch reactor. On Boston housing, SOSX training gave the best common-architecture model (test MAE 1.984, R2 = 0.925). On Williams-Otto, pruning 15% of hidden neurons reduced MILP solution time 6.5-fold while preserving the selected operating point. For the Van de Vusse reactor, a physics-informed ReLU RNN pruned from 32 to 12 hidden units enabled tractable exact MILP embedding and a 4% true-plant economic gain over the best ramp-and-hold policy, while terminal-aware shrinking-horizon prediction reached 99.7% of the direct-shooting reference. For the hydroformylation reactor, one-step exact-tanh IPOPT reached 99.2% of the certified reference product with peak pressure 20.002 bar (limit 20 bar) and 14.36 s total solve time over 280 moves; ReLU and PWL-native controllers achieved comparable recovery, with ReLU fastest. Longer horizons and physics-informed training did not consistently improve closed-loop results. Overall, compact linearized neural surrogates enable accurate, tractable optimization in steady-state and dynamic applications, with constraint fidelity identified as a key direction for improvement.

When and why can integral constraints improve the accuracy of physics-informed neural networks?

Myeong-Su Lee

Adding constraints obtained by integrating governing equations to the loss has been reported to improve the accuracy of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) in several applications, but mathematical understanding of these benefits remains limited. For several partial differential equations (PDEs), we derive stability estimates for losses with and without integral constraints. The comparison reveals that in certain parameter regimes, the standard loss has very weak control over specific error components, and that the integral constraints restore uniform control of these components. This explains when and why the constraints improve accuracy. Finally, numerical experiments support this interpretation. In parameter regimes where the standard loss is predicted to have weak control over those components, the two losses indeed show clear differences in accuracy. Conversely, in regimes where the standard loss retains sufficient control, both losses yield similar accuracy.

Stochastic counterpart of Nesterov's method for sampling and provable non-acceleration of samplers with heavy-ball structure

A. Sharma

Momentum also provides a connection with sampling, and interestingly, many kinetic Langevin samplers reduce to the heavy-ball recurrence when velocity is eliminated and noise is removed. This observation motivates a natural question: what is the corresponding sampler for Nesterov's method? We propose a sampler whose deterministic part is exactly Nesterov's recurrence, driven by temporally correlated Gaussian noise and requires one new gradient evaluation per step. Noise correlation provides $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$ bias for smooth observables. We establish geometric ergodicity for possibly non-convex potentials. For strongly convex potentials with Lipschitz continuous gradients, we obtain contraction rates and show that, up to an additive constant, the energy decays at a rate of order $κ^{-1/2}$, where $κ$ is the condition number. For Gaussian targets, we show that relaxation has square-root dependence on $κ$. We further prove that any sampler based on heavy-ball dynamics with additive noise cannot achieve accelerated convergence uniformly over the class of strongly convex functions. Finally, we compare the proposed sampler with OBABO, BAOAB, HMC, MALA, BPS, and NUTS in numerical experiments and demonstrate the better performance of proposed sampler.

Can pretrained Transformers deliver semiparametrically efficient inference?

Han Yan, Jin Zhu, Chengchun Shi, Tengyao Wang

Semiparametric models provide a broad framework for statistical inference, yet efficient estimation typically relies on influence function correction tailored to each estimand and on nuisance estimation repeated for every sample. We show that a single pretrained Transformer can deliver efficient estimation and valid inference for a family of estimands in one forward pass, without deriving efficient influence functions or estimating nuisance functions. Pretrained on simulated data with the true parameter as the only label, the Transformer learns the posterior mean and covariance of the queried estimand and hence returns an estimate and a Wald-type confidence region. Theoretically, we prove that, for locally flat pretraining priors of slowly growing complexity, the posterior mean is efficient for every queried estimand. The Transformer inherits this efficiency with asymptotically exact coverage, in probability over evaluation populations and queries, even under distribution shift.
