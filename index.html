TriGeoBench: A Multimodal Mathematical Geometry Benchmark with Figures and TikZ

Jing Wang, Yitong Yang, Hao Dou, Yaomin Wu, Shuting He, Tingfei Shen

Multimodal Large Language Models show strong vision-language capabilities, yet their geometry-centric reasoning remains insufficiently understood. Existing mathematical benchmarks typically rely on rasterized figures and cover limited geometric knowledge areas and response formats, making it difficult to assess whether models reason over geometric structures or exploit superficial visual patterns. We introduce TriGeoBench, a geometry-centric multimodal benchmark constructed from Chinese middle- and high-school mathematics examinations. TriGeoBench contains 7,000 question-answer pairs and 12,235 geometric figures with TikZ code, spanning plane geometry, solid geometry, analytic geometry, function, and vector geometry across diverse response formats. Each instance forms a {question-answer pair, image, TikZ} triplet: question-answer pairs are stored in LaTeX, images as RGB figures, and TikZ preserves geometric topology, symbolic annotations, and relational structure. We evaluate representative open- and closed-source MLLMs through image-to-TikZ and multimodal reasoning probes. Results reveal persistent fine-grained structure and image-text alignment challenges. Oracle experiments with human-curated reference TikZ suggest that accurate executable representations provide complementary geometric cues; they do not evaluate an end-to-end conversion pipeline. TriGeoBench offers a testbed for structure-aware mathematical reasoning.

Convergence of stochastic gradient descent for learning linear neural networks

Gabin Maxime Nguegnang, Bubacarr Bah, Holger Rauhut, Ulrich Terstiege

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is the cornerstone of modern deep learning, yet its theoretical convergence properties remain poorly understood in non-convex settings. To address this gap, we study SGD dynamics in deep linear neural networks - simplified models that retain key non-convex characteristics while remaining analytically tractable. Building on recent results for gradient descent, we extend convergence guarantees to SGD under regularized squared loss. We establish almost sure boundedness of SGD iterates. Using the framework of asymptotic pseudotrajectories from stochastic approximation theory, our analysis establishes then, for both deterministic and adaptive step-size choices, convergence of SGD to the set of critical points under mild conditions (without relying on Lipschitz properties or whitened data). To our knowledge, this is the first work providing almost sure convergence guarantees for SGD in deep linear networks, bridging the gap between theoretical optimization and practical stochastic training.

Nikolay Yavich, Alexander Ryabov, Viacheslav Naumov, Sayan Ranu, N. M. Anoop Krishnan, Evgeny Burnaev

Approximate solutions of partial differential equations (PDEs) on unstructured grids can be represented on a graph. Numerical optimization involving stiff evolutionary and static PDEs often requires solving a large system of equations, which can be treated as a constraint. In this work, we present a fully differentiable graph-based implicit solver that incorporates this constraint. We evaluate its performance across four key tasks: a) linear and nonlinear forward modeling of evolutionary and static problems, b) solving inverse problems to estimate equation coefficients, c) solving inverse problems to estimate the right-hand side, and d) learnable graph coarsening to accelerate forward modeling. The stability and differentiability of our solver enable significant reductions in the complexity of forward modeling while efficiently addressing related inverse problems. These capabilities make it a powerful tool for different physics and engineering applications, in particular for geophysics, where inverse problems are commonly solved in order to reduce the model uncertainty.

OptScientist: Automated Discovery of Optimization Laws

Ma ZhenYao, Yue Liang, Jian-Qiao Zhu

Many scientific phenomena are best understood as outcomes of underlying optimization problems, yet automated scientific discovery has largely focused on recovering equations that describe observed input-output patterns. We introduce OptScientist, a novel automated framework for discovering compact symbolic optimization laws directly from data. OptScientist comprises (i) a model that infers symbolic optimization problems by first learning to represent data as an optimization problem and then extracting its symbolic form, and (ii) an LLM agentic system embedding the model in a discovery process that integrates theoretical priors from scientific literature, discovers candidate laws, abstracts their underlying mechanisms, and suggests new data to collect. We first evaluate OptScientist on OptFeynman, a novel benchmark of 99 physics-derived optimization problems, where OptScientist recovers ground-truth laws for over 80% of the problems and remains robust to noisy contextual information, while substantially outperforming machine-learning, symbolic, and LLM-based model-discovery baselines. On human risky choice and central-bank monetary policy, OptScientist discovers compact symbolic optimization laws that achieve held-out predictive performance competitive with leading baselines, while using few parameters and substantially fewer tokens than LLM baselines. Overall, OptScientist extends automated scientific discovery to uncovering optimization laws that generate observed phenomena, enabling automated theory building across the physical, biological, behavioral, and social sciences.

Soliton Profiles in 1D: Enhanced Neural-Network Solvers and Constrained Deep Ritz Methods

Chandler Haight, Alex D Rodriguez, Svetlana Roudenko

We develop and compare enhanced neural-network solvers for one-dimensional ground-state profiles, which are solutions of $$ Q^{\prime\prime}=bQ-γQ^{p},\quad Q>0,\quad p>1, \quad b, γ>0, $$ the stationary profile equation for the nonlinear Schrödinger, generalized Korteweg-de Vries, and nonlinear Klein-Gordon equations. Its explicit sech-type solutions serve as exact reference for error computation and benchmark hyperparameter selection. We improve on the standard Adam-trained physics-informed neural network (PINN) baseline by introducing higher-order optimization via Gauss-Newton/Levenberg-Marquardt and L-BFGS refinements, as well as Fourier-feature PINNs, and constrained Deep Ritz (DR) formulations. In DR approaches we introduce two new neural-network formulations, which implement two classical variational characterizations: a Nehari-constrained Deep Ritz method, in which the height is learned by projecting onto the Nehari/Pokhozhaev manifold, and a Weinstein Deep Ritz method, in which a normalized profile minimizes the Weinstein quotient. The Deep Ritz formulations perform best overall, in particular, the Weinstein variant gives the best error-runtime balance. The neural-network based solvers remain less accurate and efficient than classical Petviashvili iteration in 1D, however, the current study provides a proof of concept: the mesh-free formulation, non-reliance on exact profiles or the related features make them promising in higher dimensions and for more complicated nonlinearities and dispersive operators.

Local Work, Global Progress: A Convergence Theory of AI Agent Collaboration

Ivan Ilin, Peter Richtárik

Teams of large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly applied to complex tasks, but their collaboration protocols are mostly designed empirically, without convergence guarantees. We formalize collaborative problem solving as block optimization: each agent improves one part of a shared solution, guided by its local objective plus a correction for how its changes affect the other parts. A coordinator checks every proposal against the global objective and merges proposals that do not interfere with each other. We prove linear convergence up to an error floor that vanishes when the corrections are exact. Interfering proposals can also be merged once their combination has been checked; the rate then depends on how well the proposals actually combine, not on worst-case interactions. With GPT-OSS-120B agents on robot routing, a three-agent team finds collision-free plans in every run, better than those of a single agent that controls all robots. On a larger routing task, a six-agent team completes its search about twice as fast as the single agent (in median). Merging the best checked combination of proposals, rather than only proposals that do not interfere, cuts the team's search time by about 30%. With constants measured on the runs, our convergence bound holds in every round and follows the observed progress. We also test the theory on query planning in SQLite and on a synthetic task.

Corrected Block Sparse Spectral Optimization: Exact Models, Finite Identification, and Local Dynamics

Omid Shams Solari

The block generalized-power method pairs threshold-based support selection with inexpensive Stiefel updates, but the standard block formulation conflates two nonequivalent objectives and leaves inactive components, threshold ties, and post-selection refitting ill-specified. We rebuild the model from the primal. Eliminating each loading column from the normalized loading-ball problem gives a sum of soft-thresholded Euclidean norms (A-SUM); the squared-threshold objective in common use arises from a quadratic lift (A-SQ). We prove both identities, exhibit a two-component instance whose maximizers disagree, and specify explicit conventions for zero-response columns, ties, rank deficiency, and mask-before-refit. For A-SQ we separate the historical full-polar iteration from a safeguarded Riemannian ascent and prove, for the latter, whole-sequence convergence via a sufficient-ascent lemma, a relative-error lemma, and the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz property, which applies because the soft-threshold objective and the Stiefel constraint are semi-algebraic. A strict threshold margin then forces finite identification of the support and sign pattern. The post-identification map is ordinary power iteration on the active covariance for a rank-one hard threshold, and the Frechet derivative of a coupled polar map for component-specific supports; a radius-based safe-screening rule follows from the same local analysis. Planted-spike experiments match the predicted identification time and local rates. On modern-AI panels (GPT-2 residual stream, ViT CLS stream, Pitprops) the thresholded block-sparse components match dense PCA on reconstruction and improve on it in monosemanticity and class specialisation. On three native-label scRNA datasets (Zheng17 FACS-sorted PBMCs, 68k donor-A with FACS-correlated labels, Tabula Muris) A-SQ beats dense PCA on cluster-recovery AMI by +0.023 to +0.044 at matched sparsity.

We hope that VibeTraining-Bench will serve as a unified platform for advancing RSI, deepening our understanding of cross-component optimization for agent systems. Code and data are available at https://github.com/D2I-ai/VibeTraining-Bench.

Maestro: Agentic Formal Proof Development by Coordination

Haoyu Li, Zeren Shui, Yuhao Zhang, Ferhat Erata, Benjamin Mikek, Murali Krishna Ramanathan

Lean is increasingly used to verify mathematical proofs and software produced by LLM agents, but writing formal proofs remains hard. Existing prover systems take a divide-and-conquer approach that recursively decomposes hard proof obligations into easier lemmas and splits again any lemma that resists proof. However, a lemma can resist proof for different reasons, including insufficient guidance, a false statement, or a flaw in the decomposition that produced it. Existing systems cannot tell these apart as a failed attempt reports little beyond its failure, and responses are confined to the failing node without considering the rest of the proof or revising the plan above it. The system may thus keep decomposing obligations along a flawed proof direction until the budget runs out, causing the whole proof attempt to fail. We introduce Maestro, a Lean prover harness that handles failures through global coordination. Provers report why they failed, not just that they failed, and a coordinator that sees the whole proof chooses an appropriate response, such as guiding a stuck prover, correcting a false lemma, or retracting a flawed subtree and replanning it. The same harness extends to verified code synthesis, where a checked counterexample sends the code back for repair. We evaluate Maestro with four frontier LLMs on seven mathematics and three software verification benchmarks. It proves more targets than each model's native coding agent (Claude Code or Codex) on every benchmark the agent has not already saturated, with the largest gains on research-level mathematics. With Claude Opus 5, it discovers its own proofs of 34 of the 48 theorems in the June 2026 release of arXivLean, against 19 for the native agent. It also outperforms all published prover systems on every benchmark they share, solving 669/672 PutnamBench, 59/60 Lean-IMO-Bench, and 6/6 IMO 2026.

We treat three asymptotic regimes systematically. First, the small-noise limit for stochastic differential equations and the large-volume limit for Markov jump processes, where the Large Deviation Principle emerges from the Law of Large Numbers through progressively finer characterizations of fluctuations. Second, the long-time limit combined with small noise, yielding the quasipotential formalism for metastability and transition rates in both equilibrium and nonequlibrium systems. Third, the long-time limit at finite noise, connecting to spectral methods via tilted generators and enabling reduction of multiscale systems.We survey computational approaches including minimum action methods, their geometric variants for quasipotential calculations, and neural network techniques. The Schlögl model of chemical bistability serves as a running example, illustrating both scalar and spatially extended settings. Appendices provide self-contained background on stochastic calculus and Markov jump processes.

Conformal Prediction-Enabled Sequence-level Policy Optimization for Reasoning Language Models

Hui Yang, Wan Tian, Tao Ren, Zhongyi Li, Yijie Peng

Reinforcement learning improves the reasoning capability of large language models through verifiable rewards for mathematical and coding tasks. In token-level methods such as GRPO, clipping acts on individual token ratios while the reward and advantage apply to a complete response. Recent sequence-level methods align these units but still use fixed clipping bands, whose tail probabilities change as the score distribution evolves. We propose Conformal Prediction-Enabled Sequence-level Policy Optimization (CSPO), which adaptively calibrates sequence-level clipping bounds using recent training statistics. The resulting band targets a specified marginal tail budget and adapts to policy drift; calibration conditional on response length requires additional assumptions. We bound the probability that a fresh score falls outside the band by the target miscoverage level plus explicit CDF discrepancy and drift terms. Under additional moment conditions, this bound also controls the objective and gradient error relative to the unclipped GSPO power surrogate. Experiments with the Qwen3-1.7B series show improvements over GSPO on difficult mathematical reasoning benchmarks and ALFWorld, with clipping endpoints that adapt during training.

Norm-optimal model order reduction in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

Tamás Dózsa, Andrea Angino, Rolf Krause, Matthias Voigt

We consider the problem of model order reduction (MOR) in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs). We prove generalizations of well-known necessary interpolatory conditions for norm-optimal reduced-order models, originally stated for rational functions in $H_2$ Hardy-Hilbert spaces. We construct an alternative to the iterative rational Krylov (IRKA) algorithm for RKHSs with differentiable kernels to obtain norm-optimal reduced-order models. Then, we introduce generalized Malmquist-Takenaka (GMT) functions that can be used to define orthonormal bases in an arbitrary RKHS. Using GMT functions, we propose unitary transformations between separable RKHSs. This allows us to extend our optimality conditions and subsequent algorithms to MOR problems posed in RKHSs with non-differentiable kernels. The potential and versatility of the proposed MOR framework is demonstrated in several numerical experiments. We perform norm-optimal MOR on various machine learning models such as support vector machines and deep ReLU networks. In addition, we show how the proposed MOR framework can be used to solve seemingly unrelated problems. In particular, we provide an example for fitting high degree polynomials to data and also obtain a FEM-like method for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) with an optimal kernel basis.

Theorem prover Arend

Fedor Part, Valery Isaev, Sergey Sinchuk

This paper is an exposition of the theorem prover Arend developed at JetBrains Research. Arend language has been designed to facilitate the formalization of mathematics within the framework of univalent foundations and with the focus on constructive mathematics. It is based on a variant of homotopy type theory with cubical syntax that is more closely aligned with Martin-Löf Type Theory (MLTT) than fully computational cubical type theories, while still retaining some of their computational properties useful in practice. Among the most notable features of the Arend language is its powerful system of classes and records supporting subsumptive subtyping, partial implementations and anonymous extensions, which provides extensive flexibility for constructing hierarchies of definitions. Formalization in Arend is streamlined by the IntelliJ Arend plugin, which transforms IntelliJ IDEA into a full-fledged integrated development environment (IDE) for the Arend language. The main Arend library arend-lib includes formalizations in constructive algebra, topology and synthetic homotopy theory.

Martin Hernandez, Xinyan Qing, Mo Zhou, Yuhua Zhu

Continuous-time reinforcement learning seeks an optimal policy under unknown dynamics driven by stochastic differential equations using observations collected at discrete times. We develop a policy gradient framework that combines rigorous convergence analysis with learning from discrete data. We first establish a performance difference lemma and derive policy gradient formulas for randomized and parametrized policies. We then prove exponential convergence of an entropy-regularized policy flow to the regularized optimum. For discrete parametrized updates, we derive a convergence bound that explicitly separates the contributions of critic error, policy-regression error, and the learning step size. To enable learning under unknown dynamics, we integrate this framework with PhiBE, which constructs a continuous-time surrogate generator from discrete transition data. The resulting on-policy and off-policy model-free actor-critic algorithms preserve the continuous-time structure of the control problem. Numerical experiments on an entropy-regularized linear-quadratic regulator and a nonlinear wheeled robot demonstrate the performance and stability of the proposed methods with both known and data-driven dynamics.

Low Residuals, Wrong Value: Learning the Viscosity Solution of Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs Equations with PINNs

Raul Jimenez, Pablo Tejerina-Pérez, Leonid Sarieddine, Pedro Tarancón-Álvarez, Marisol Traforetti, Pau Solé-Vilaró

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and other residual-trained solvers judge a candidate solution by the size of its sampled PDE residual. For first-order Hamilton-Jacobi equations this is not sufficient: the same equation and boundary data admit Lipschitz almost-everywhere solutions that differ from the viscosity solution by $O(1)$ while violating the equation only on sets of measure zero. We study whether residual minimization selects such solutions in a fixed-horizon pursuit-evasion game whose Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs value function is known in closed form on the circle $S^1$ and on the flat tori $\mathbb{T}^{2}$ and $\mathbb{T}^{4}$. We train four neural parameterizations with ten seeds on each geometry, 120 runs in total. A standard network with periodic Fourier features converges to folded almost-everywhere solutions in all 30 runs: small residuals and the expected capture-layer gradient, yet values that are wrong over most of the domain. A network with periodicity imposed through the loss recovers the viscosity solution in one and two dimensions but becomes unreliable in four. Two parameterizations that receive the geodesic distance do substantially better: a single network fed the wrapped displacement and its distance recovers the viscosity solution in all 30 runs, while a minimum over periodic image charts succeeds in 27 of 30 and introduces a wrong-image failure mode. Finally, for the convex Hamiltonian considered here, we use the semiconcavity of the viscosity solution to build a mesh-free finite-difference test that needs only forward network evaluations; it detects the folded solutions and the harmful min-of-charts failures without access to the exact value function. A small sampled residual thus does not by itself validate a learned Hamilton-Jacobi solution; the parameterization and independent structural diagnostics can be essential for selecting the viscosity solution.

Distributional Lifting for Discrete Optimization

Ningji Wei, Jiaming Liang

We introduce a framework that transforms discrete optimization into continuous optimization over parameterized probability distributions. The framework reveals a common distributional structure behind several methods that have largely developed independently, including continuous reformulations for multilinear binary optimization, Markov decision processes and reinforcement learning, the cross-entropy method, mean-field variational optimization, and the quantum approximate optimization algorithm. It enables flexible and modular algorithm design, for example, by using neural networks to incorporate contextual information or by enforcing convexity of the lifted problem. We establish two theoretical results: a link between the lifted optimality gap and sampling quality in the original discrete problem, and a convergence guarantee for the lifted optimization problem under suitable conditions. These results lead to a unified algorithmic template based on batched sampling and stochastic gradient updates that is naturally compatible with GPU computation. We demonstrate the framework through three case studies spanning multidimensional knapsack, covering-constrained three-dimensional assignment, and a stochastic mixed-integer location-allocation problem, showing that it can accommodate distinct combinatorial structures and scale to large discrete and mixed-integer settings.

Newton-Based Methods for Bilevel Optimization Beyond Lower-Level Strong Convexity

Youran Dong, Junfeng Yang, Wei Yao, Jin Zhang

Bilevel optimization provides a framework for machine learning tasks such as hyperparameter optimization, data reweighting, and meta-learning. Existing Newton-based bilevel methods typically rely on the classical hypergradient formula, which is generally unavailable when lower-level solutions are nonunique and the lower-level Hessian is singular. We develop two Newton-based methods beyond lower-level strong convexity by exploiting curvature shared by the lower-level and multiplier updates. Newton-based GALET handles potentially singular lower-level curvature under a positive spectral-gap condition, exploiting its range structure to avoid the squared-Hessian multiplier system used in GALET. Newton-based BAMM uses a strongly convex aggregation model when the lower-level objective is convex and the upper-level objective is strongly convex in the lower-level variable. We establish convergence guarantees and improved complexity bounds relative to GALET and sl-BAMM under the corresponding assumptions. We further introduce GNBO, a generalized Newton-based framework that captures the shared-curvature structure of the proposed methods and several existing Newton and quasi-Newton bilevel methods. Experiments on synthetic problems, data hyper-cleaning, and GPT-2 data reweighting evaluate the proposed methods across different bilevel learning tasks.

Confessions of a Masked MDP

Sebastian Reboul, Lorenzo Croissant, Hélène Halconruy

In reinforcement learning, one presumes the action set to be known ahead of time, and, thus, one must know if any state-action pairs are not permissible ahead of computations. In some applications, determining this action mask is the main computational bottleneck. Isolating this problem to highlight its core properties, we study how to combine the search for an optimal policy with the learning of the mask itself, under a linear MDP model in which each attempted action reveals the exact value of a linear score determining its validity. In this framework, we first construct an algorithm based on deterministic optimistic optimization and show that it is minimax optimal, which we show is also computationally efficient. However, it is sub-optimal on non-degenerate instances, as we show. We propose an alternative, which is also computationally efficient, which plans its exploration strategy. This algorithm admits a formal interpretation as an analogue of information-directed-sampling for deterministic feedback, which may be of independent interest. Finally, we study an application of this framework to transfer learning, where the invalid action set changes over time.

ReuF2F: Benchmarking Coding Agents on Formalized Undergraduate Research Problems

Zeyu Zheng, Yiyang Liu, Yuyang Zhao, Congmin Zheng, Jiachen Zhu, Jianian Chen

As AI systems begin to resolve open research problems, reliable verification becomes increasingly important. Formal mathematics provides a trusted framework for mathematical reasoning, enabling agents to produce machine-checkable proofs while conducting mathematical exploration. Existing formal mathematics benchmarks often focus on known results or a small set of prominent open problems, which can become difficult to evaluate reliably once solutions are discovered and widely disseminated. Existing evaluation protocols are also not always aligned with how modern coding agents operate. We therefore introduce ReuF2F, a Lean 4 benchmark of 244 formalized open problems drawn from Research Experiences for Undergraduates (REU), spanning entry-level open research problems across a broad range of mathematical subfields. We expect these less prominent open problems, which tend to attract less attention and may remain unsolved for longer, to be better suited for held-out evaluation of end-to-end open problem resolution in Lean. We adopt a standard coding-agent evaluation setting, placing each problem and its logical negation into a Lean project in a Docker container. We also build Leanimum-agent on mini-SWE-agent to provide a unified harness for fair comparisons across models. Our evaluation finds that even the best-performing model resolves only ~40% of the problems, leaving substantial room for improvement on formalized open research problems.
