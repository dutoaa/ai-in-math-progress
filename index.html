Structural functional identifiability of unknown differential equation observation maps

Torkel E Loman, Alexander P Browning, Ruth E Baker

In many differential equation models of real-world systems, the mechanistic structure of the dynamics is known, but the map from the dynamics to the observations is not. This is potentially problematic, as it can prevent us from e.g. learning model parameter values from data. Examples where this occurs include measuring a fluorescent marker as a proxy for the dynamics of a cellular circuit, and using wastewater measurements to follow the development of an epidemic. The nascent field of hybrid modelling, in which machine learning components (like neural networks) are embedded within mechanistic models, provides the tools to learn such maps. Here, the system's dynamics keep their mechanistic form, while a neural network represents the unknown observation map and is trained alongside any unknown core system parameters. This raises a question of identifiability: when can the true observation map be learnt, and when does one simply learn some map that transforms the dynamics into the available data? Building on previous work, we show how structural functional identifiability analysis can establish whether a model's unknown observation maps and core system parameters are identifiable, i.e. can be uniquely learnt from data. We both prove general results on when models with unknown observation maps are, and are not, identifiable, and demonstrate a general methodology (based on differential algebra) for assessing the identifiability of such models. This paves the way for applying classic differential equation workflows to the many real-world systems that contain such unknown maps.

Balancing Exploration and Exploitation in Constrained Combinatorial Optimization with U(1)-Symmetric Tensor Trains

Sergei Iudin, Mikhail Podobrii, Dmitry Zheltkov, Stanislav Moiseev

Tensor Train (TT) decomposition has emerged as a powerful tool for mitigating the curse of dimensionality in a variety of machine learning and combinatorial optimization tasks. Recent advances show that TT-based generative models can efficiently represent and explore high-dimensional discrete spaces, even when the cost function structure is unknown. However, incorporating hard linear constraints into TT-based optimization remains challenging, as existing interpolation and sampling approaches often fail when feasible points are rare. In this work, we develop a general TT-based framework for constrained combinatorial optimization built upon U(1)-symmetric tensor networks. Our method introduces an expressibility-enhancement mechanism for constructing sparse symmetric tensors and a graph-based diversity measure that guides sample selection during training. On assignment benchmarks, expressibility enhancement and diversity-aware selection improve mean solution quality over their respective baselines. The assignment-selected diversifying strategy also extends to nonpolynomial Set Partitioning, with larger relative gains observed in the higher-tensor-rank data families. Separate runtime benchmarks show substantial speedups over ConstrainTNet and PROTES on non-degenerate assignment workloads; comparisons with simulated annealing depend on constraint information and computational budget, but, overall, support the practicality of the current implementation.

A Convergence Analysis of the Random Feature Method for Linear PDEs

Jingrun Chen, Yixin Luo, Xiaojun Peng

The Random Feature Method (RFM) distinguishes itself among machine learning approaches to partial differential equations (PDEs) through its superior accuracy and computational efficiency. Nevertheless, the theoretical underpinnings of RFM remain incompletely elucidated. This paper provides a comprehensive analysis of RFM within the framework of kernel methods, focusing on two principal aspects: (i) approximation by finitely many random features in Sobolev norms and the PDE residual seminorm, and (ii) estimation over the induced reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) from finitely many noiseless collocation observations. We relate both problems to kernel ridge regression (KRR) and establish upper and lower bounds in terms of the eigenvalues of the associated integral operators under the respective assumptions. Combining the approximation and estimation lower bounds, we establish a lower bound under simultaneous restrictions on the numbers of random features and collocation observations. We further quantify how activation regularity affects these eigenvalues, obtaining algebraic upper bounds for finitely smooth activations and stretched-exponential upper bounds for analytic activations.

Rethinking Supervision Scaling in On-Policy Distillation: From Dense Supervision to Stochastic Horizons

Xinhao Yao, Zhenxing Dou, Yong Liu

On-policy distillation (OPD) provides dense token-level supervision along student-generated trajectories, yet the learning benefits of longer trajectories may not scale proportionally with their computational cost. We investigate how supervision utility evolves with trajectory length. Controlled experiments reveal diminishing returns from additional supervised tokens, while showing that later positions remain learnable and exhibit substantial cross-position supervision transfer. Gradient analysis further identifies shared update components across response positions and diverse position-specific residuals whose cross-rollout consistency decreases at greater depths. Motivated by these findings, we propose Stochastic Horizons On-Policy Distillation (SH-OPD), which reduces redundant supervision by randomly varying rollout horizons while preserving access to deeper response states. SH-OPD samples a shared horizon for each training batch, enabling efficient parallel generation without per-sequence horizon scheduling overhead. We characterize the resulting reweighting bias and derive a compute-reweighting trade-off under standard optimization assumptions. Experiments on mathematical reasoning and code generation demonstrate that SH-OPD achieves slightly higher mathematical reasoning accuracy at approximately 45% of the per-update cost of standard OPD, while maintaining competitive code generation performance at around 60% of the baseline cost. These results highlight the benefits of efficient supervision allocation across response depths.

Regularized Score Centering

Hao Bai, Chenlu Ye, Xuanchang Zhang, Aviral Kumar, Tong Zhang

Asynchronous reinforcement learning increases training throughput, but data from stale policies and numerical mismatch between training and inference can destabilize learning. To stabilize off-policy learning, score centering (SC) removes the effect of reward baselines on the expected update. Our gradient diagnostics show less extreme gradients with SC than with importance sampling (IS), but SC can still fail when the learner moves far from the behavior policy that generated the data. To limit this policy mismatch, we introduce Regularized Score Centering (RSC), which adds a reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty to encourage the learner's next-token distribution to stay close to the behavior policy's next-token distribution. RSC works with or without truncated importance sampling (TIS), which caps token importance weights. Our theoretical analysis supports this approach, showing that KL regularization improves convergence guarantees. We evaluate dense and mixture-of-experts models on mathematical reasoning and agentic tasks, including online shopping, tool use, games, and fully asynchronous software engineering. RSC with TIS substantially improves sample efficiency across all agentic benchmarks, attaining higher training rewards with the same number of sampled responses. In final evaluations, it achieves top performance across all six agentic tasks, improving by 14.3% on AppWorld and reaching 9.9 times the strongest baseline's score on BabyAI.

Second scaling regime in high-dimensional function approximation and curse of dimensionality

Shukai Du, Thu Le, Samuel N. Stechmann

High-dimensional function approximation is often subject to the curse of dimensionality, commonly described by a hypercube scaling regime with a large expense of $R^d$ grid points, where $R$ denotes the side length of a hypercube in $d$ dimensions. Here we show that a second scaling regime, which we call the lattice ball scaling regime, also arises and is relevant for practical applications. In this regime, the expense scales as $d^{R^2}/(R^2!)$ and therefore grows polynomially with $d$. This regime arises without additional special assumptions of special types of function spaces or error norms. The lattice ball scaling regime occurs when $R^2\ll d$, due to the discrete nature of counting the expense as the number of lattice points inside a hypersphere. Here we investigate this regime, which arises from the classical problem of approximating high-dimensional functions from common spaces of analytic or $m$-times continuously differentiable functions using orthogonal series (spectral method) such as Fourier, Chebyshev, and Legendre polynomial series. For many applications, the expense in the lattice ball scaling regime can be substantially smaller than that of the hypercube scaling regime, making accurate approximations more accessible. Numerical illustrations include classifying images of handwritten digits from the MNIST dataset and learning the solution operators of partial differential equations, such as Burgers' equation and the radiative transfer equation. The potential for connections with artificial neural networks and other machine learning methods is also discussed.

Stochastic Representation-Inspired Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Incompressible Fluid Flows

Zihao Guo, Zhongmin Qian

The effectiveness of neural networks in solving partial differential equations depends not only on their approximation capability but also on the suitability of their representations for the underlying solutions. Motivated by stochastic integral representations of incompressible fluid flows and the associated Biot--Savart kernel, we propose a Biot--Savart-informed (i.e. physics-informed) neural network (BS-PINN) for the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations. By extracting a rational profile from a regularized Biot--Savart kernel, we construct a smooth activation function with fixed and learnable kernel scales. Both variants retain the conventional velocity--pressure formulation and physics-informed training framework, allowing the influence of the activation function to be examined independently. We evaluate the method on five benchmarks, including Kovasznay flow, cylinder wake, three-dimensional Beltrami flow, Taylor--Green vortex, and double shear layer. Across 21 benchmark--architecture comparisons, both variants consistently reduce the combined velocity error relative to the conventional $\tanh$-based PINN, achieving mean relative error reductions of 31.7\% and 33.2\%, respectively. Improvements are also observed in reported pressure and vorticity errors. Notably, the double-shear-layer experiments demonstrate improved resolution of localized shear structures and, for BS-PINN-F, qualitatively clearer vortex-roll-up patterns in the long-time simulation. These findings highlight the potential of incorporating analytical flow structures into neural-network representations for incompressible flow simulations.

MCTS Is Counting the Wrong Thing: Learning Exploration Counts for LLM Reasoning

Xuan Zhang, Zhijian Zhou, Zicheng Hu, Yan Feng, Hongyan Hao, Yuan Qi

Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) has powered landmark successes in reinforcement learning, yet its gains in LLM reasoning can remain modest despite substantial search effort. We identify an overlooked bottleneck: what MCTS counts. Standard UCT assigns independent visit counts to textual branches, allowing different formulations of the same reasoning strategy to repeatedly receive exploration bonuses. Our theoretical analysis shows that this count fragmentation can impair exploration efficiency, as quantified by increased cumulative regret. To address this problem, we introduce Learned Pseudo-Count MCTS (LPC-MCTS), which changes only the exploration statistic in UCT. A lightweight Coin-Flipping Network head on the policy's hidden representations estimates representation coverage, enabling related branches to share exploration evidence. Across 5 mathematical-reasoning and 7 search benchmarks, LPC-MCTS consistently outperforms strong MCTS baselines. Holding each LPC-MCTS-trained policy fixed, replacing standard MCTS counts at inference improves average mean@16 accuracy across 5 mathematical benchmarks by 3.79 percentage points on Qwen2.5-7B and 2.71 points on Qwen3-4B. Applying LPC-MCTS during both training and inference yields gains of 4.96 and 4.37 points over standard MCTS, respectively. Together, our theory and experiments show that changing what MCTS counts can substantially improve its exploration efficiency and reasoning performance.

Large Language Models as Nonlinear Dynamical Systems: The Three Clocks of Depth, Training, and Generation

Maor Farid

Language models are dynamical systems. A transformer is iterated over its layers, over the steps of its training and over the tokens it generates, and on each of these three clocks it shows behavior that nonlinear dynamics has studied for a century: fixed points, bifurcations, synchronization, chaos and noise-driven escape from potential wells. The two communities rarely meet, so this behavior is often rediscovered under new names. We build a bridge between them. To describe, we condense about 200 papers into the Three-Clock Dictionary, which maps fifteen concepts of nonlinear dynamics onto each clock and marks every correspondence as proved, measured or conjectured, and we show each concept in a mechanical system and in a language model side by side. To explain, we derive reduced-order models: the softmax is a Boltzmann distribution, gradient descent an Euler step on a nonlinear spring, a loss spike a ball thrown out of a stiff valley, attention on the circle a Kuramoto model, and a repetition loop a well that deepens while it is occupied. We then test these explanations on open language models, on a laptop CPU. Escape from repetition loops in GPT-2 and Pythia follows the Arrhenius law with the logit gap as the barrier, and the barrier deepens and then saturates, so the temperature needed to leave a loop falls like $1/\ln N$ with the generation length $N$ and rises with model size. Coherent text lives in a narrow window of temperatures, which a small reasoning model shares at its recommended temperature. Training makes each Pythia layer more expansive, and a small transformer trains at a multi-mode edge of stability. The third step, solving problems of language models, is still ahead: controllers derived from the escape analysis remove loops but do not yet beat the best fixed temperature. We state twelve open problems where nonlinear dynamics could get there.

PISG: Constraint-Aligned Signal Amplification for Diffusion-Based Combinatorial Optimization

Yuming Zhang, Xianchen Zhou, Hongxia Wang

Diffusion-based solvers have become a prominent approach to neural combinatorial optimization. At inference time, they assign each decision variable a prediction score. A greedy decoder ranks candidates by these scores and checks hard constraints to ensure feasibility. Top-ranked candidates should be both promising and jointly feasible, making prediction-constraint alignment critical. When this alignment is weak, greedy decoding skips constraint-violating candidates and falls back to lower-ranked alternatives, degrading solution quality. Existing test-time methods improve search, sampling, or decoding, but do not directly strengthen this alignment before decoding. We observe that trained denoisers already carry constraint-aligned structural signals. Building on this observation, we propose Perturbed Instance-Structure Guidance (PISG), a training-free method that amplifies these signals at test time. At each denoising step, PISG constructs a structure-degraded prediction and extrapolates the unperturbed prediction away from it. The resulting guided prediction improves candidate rankings before constrained decoding. Signal analysis shows that this guidance concentrates on constraint-critical variables rather than uniformly rescaling scores. Under greedy decoding and without downstream refinement, PISG improves all 16 TSP solver-scale pairs across DIFUSCO, T2T, FastT2T, and StruDiCO, and all four MIS-ER solvers, with MIS-ER solved-cost gains up to +4.8%. The gains persist on most large-scale settings after 2-opt, and a preliminary study extends PISG to a Transformer-based solver. These results identify prediction-constraint alignment as an effective test-time enhancement direction for diffusion-based combinatorial optimization. Code is available at https://github.com/Ming-321/PISG.

Variationally correct physics-informed operator learning for nonlinear partial differential equations

Yuan Qiu, Wolfgang Dahmen, Peng Chen

Physics-informed operator learning trains neural operators on the residual of a parametric partial differential equation (PDE) instead of labeled solution data, but its reliability depends on a variationally correct residual: one that is equivalent to the error in a norm dictated by the equation, the PDE-compliant norm. For nonlinear equations written as first-order systems, we prove that the first-order system least-squares residual is equivalent to the error in this norm near a solution branch if the linearized residual is stable and the residual derivative is Lipschitz continuous, possibly in a norm stronger than the error norm. We verify these conditions for monotone reaction, solution- and gradient-dependent diffusion, and compressible Neo-Hookean hyperelasticity. The stability mechanisms vary across these classes, as do the regions where the equivalence holds, from arbitrary bounded sets to a neighborhood of the branch. In a conforming finite element space, we show that the loss excess of a state near the finite element solution is equivalent to its squared distance from that solution. We use this loss to train a reduced basis neural operator with values in this space, with solution snapshots needed only for the basis and the loss and its symbolically derived gradient assembled by the finite element library. At inference, this loss estimates the error. Numerically, the square root of the loss excess tracks the error within factors comparable to those of the linear case, and the operator achieves higher accuracy in the PDE-compliant norm than grid-based baselines evaluated in the same finite element space.
