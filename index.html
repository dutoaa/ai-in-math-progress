At the Limits of Computability: Steps Towards Autonomous AI Discovery on the Busy Beaver Frontier

Marco Dos Santos, Vivek Veeriah, Alex Havrilla, Andrew Bolt, Goran Zuzic, Satinder Singh

Recent breakthroughs in AI for mathematics---ranging from Olympiad medal performances to resolving long-standing open conjectures---have rapidly expanded what automated systems can achieve. Yet these successes primarily concern predefined, human-formulated problems, leaving open how AI systems can autonomously uncover mathematical structure and formulate new questions. To push beyond static challenges, we explore the Busy Beaver (BB) domain, an uncomputable frontier that is fundamentally open-ended. Because no algorithm can compute the Busy Beaver number $\mathrm{BB}(n)$ for every $n$, this domain provides a natural setting for sustained exploration through mathematical conjecture and proof. In this paper, we introduce a Gemini-based agentic pipeline that automatically discovers accelerations of Turing machines, expressed as compact mathematical rules summarizing many execution steps. Our system integrates pattern recognition, automated conjecturing, informal mathematical reasoning, and formal theorem proving in Lean. With this framework, our system autonomously discovered and formally verified novel Collatz-like accelerations for eleven machines in $\mathrm{BB}(6)$. Subject to community acceptance, we provisionally classify these machines as Cryptids, namely machines whose blank-tape executions admit exact, compact mathematical descriptions but whose halting behavior remains unresolved. These results advance the analysis of $\mathrm{BB}(6)$ holdouts on the Busy Beaver frontier and demonstrate the potential of autonomous AI systems for open-ended mathematical discovery in fundamental computer science.

Functional Subnetwork Localization for Efficient Continual Learning in Large Language Models

Shuoran Jiang, Wentao Zhu, Yang Xiang, Dongfeng Li, Yunan Zhang, Mengchen Zhao

Biological intelligence continually adapts to new information while retaining prior skills through localized plasticity. In contrast, continual learning in large language models (LLMs) remains vulnerable to catastrophic forgetting. A promising direction is to identify functionally specialized subnetworks within LLMs that enable targeted and resource-efficient adaptation. Using gradient-attribution profiling, we localize cognition-associated functional subnetworks exhibiting extremely low mutual overlap. Building on this finding, we propose Function-isolated Memory-Efficient Optimization (CiMEO), a continual learning framework that routes each training example to its corresponding functional subnetwork, restricting parameter updates and optimizer states to selected regions. Across 1.4 million sequential instructions, CiMEO achieves the highest average performance among the compared methods on five generative benchmarks spanning programming, examination-style tasks, and mathematical reasoning, while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Subspace-overlap and principal-angle analyses further indicate that localized updates help preserve pre-trained representations, highlighting functional subnetwork localization as a practical approach to efficient and stable continual learning in LLMs.

On the possibilities and limitations of LLM-based multi-agent systems for multi-criteria decision-making in healthcare operations management

Marco Boresta, Tommaso Giovannelli, Sepehr Namirad, Giulia Riccardi

Multi-criteria models for healthcare operations need weights that express how stakeholders trade off patient waiting time, provider workload, and operating cost, but these weights are difficult to quantify. Teams of large language model (LLM) agents, each prompted to represent a stakeholder, have been used to supply them. We propose a framework for assessing the reliability of LLM-generated preferences as inputs to multi-objective optimization and quantifying how their uncertainty affects decisions. We study explicit preferences by asking agents to rate the importance of the criteria in an outpatient appointment scheduling problem, and implicit preferences by inferring trade-offs from the agents' pairwise choices between emergency department configurations. The computational experiments show that the explicit preferences respond to the clinic's operating conditions and differ across roles, but deliberation among the agents can erase these differences, whereas simple averaging preserves them. The pairwise choices used to reveal the agents' implicit preferences depend largely on which option is presented first, and we find limited evidence that a statistical model using contextual information alongside presentation order predicts the agents' choices on new comparison pairs better than a model using presentation order alone. We then formulate robust weighted-sum optimization models to account for preference uncertainty. In most cases, these models select different schedules or configurations from those obtained using average preference weights. These results indicate that LLM-generated preferences need an audit of this kind before they are used as optimization weights in healthcare decision support.

Recursive Gradient Retraction Methods for Stochastic Nonconvex Optimization on Stiefel Manifolds

Zhijie Yuan, Lei Sun, Ganzhao Yuan

Nonconvex optimization on Stiefel manifolds underlies many problems in machine learning and deep learning. However, existing stochastic Riemannian retraction methods often rely on stepsizes that require prior knowledge of the gradient Lipschitz constant, a prescribed iteration horizon, or restrictive batch-size choices. We develop {\sf RGR}, a recursive gradient retraction method for stochastic nonconvex optimization on Stiefel manifolds. Instead of recursively updating gradient estimators in changing tangent spaces, {\sf RGR} constructs them in the Euclidean space and subsequently maps them to tangent space, thereby avoiding vector transport. The method comprises three variants: {\sf RGR-SPIDER} for finite-sum optimization and {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} for online stochastic optimization. {\sf RGR-SPIDER} is Lipschitz-free and horizon-free, as its adaptive stepsize requires neither prior knowledge of the gradient Lipschitz constant nor a prescribed iteration horizon. {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} are mini-batch and horizon-free methods whose convergence guarantees do not rely on prescribed large batch sizes and whose parameters can be chosen without prior knowledge of the total iteration budget. We show that {\sf RGR-SPIDER} reaches an $\varepsilon$-critical point using $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})$ iterations and $\mathcal{O}(N+\sqrt{N}\varepsilon^{-2})$ component-gradient evaluations, where $N$ denotes the number of component functions. For the online setting, {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} achieve iteration complexities of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-3})$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-4})$, respectively. Experiments on principal component analysis, low-rank matrix completion, and orthogonal convolutional neural networks demonstrate faster empirical convergence than methods that construct recursive gradient estimators directly in Riemannian tangent spaces.

Computing Fréchet on Unknown Submanifolds of Riemannian Manifolds

Ian McPherson, Mauro Maggioni

Empirical averages are fundamental statistical primitives. On nonlinear spaces, their generalization, the Fréchet mean, generally lacks a closed-form solution, and its computation requires solving an optimization problem. We study the computation of intrinsic Fréchet means for data supported on an unknown compact $d$-dimensional Riemannian manifold $M_{1}$ embedded in an ambient metric space $(X,d_{\operatorname{obs}})$. Such settings arise when observations are better represented in non-Euclidean spaces, like probability distributions in Wasserstein space. We assume access only to a local sampler on $M_{1}$ and the observation metric $d_{\operatorname{obs}}$, without knowledge of its intrinsic geometry. We propose a graph-based iterative algorithm that adaptively samples the manifold and approximates geodesic interpolation. Under mild assumptions, we establish an $O(1/T)$ expected objective gap guarantee using at most $\widetilde{O}(T^{4d+3})$ sampled points. As a byproduct of our analysis we extend the metric equivalence guarantees of ISOMAP from Euclidean to Riemannian ambient spaces, which may be of independent interest. Numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of our method for computing intrinsic averages on unknown data manifolds.

Bias Control and Injection for Multi-Agent Aggregation

Sungjae Lee, Soheil Abbasloo, Jabin Koo, Jungseul Ok

Multi-agent reasoning with large language models (LLMs) raises a central question for collective decision-making: when agents disagree, whose decision should be trusted? Resolving such disagreements is difficult because agents' decisions are susceptible to non-semantic biases such as majority pressure and response-style preferences, which can obscure the quality of the underlying reasoning. Our analysis shows how bias can be both mitigated and exploited: bias control improves initial answer selection, while bias injection elicits decision instability that serves as a reliability signal. Building on these findings, we introduce a test-time aggregation framework that requires neither validation data nor prior knowledge of relative model strength. The framework implements bias control by presenting one style-normalized reasoning trace per distinct answer, and bias injection by placing each aggregating agent's initially selected answer in the minority. Overall, our framework achieves higher average accuracy and better token efficiency than competing non-oracle aggregation methods across three mathematical reasoning benchmarks and four heterogeneous LLM combinations. It also improves final-answer accuracy when applied to multi-agent debate and Mixture-of-Agents, without modifying their interaction or synthesis processes.

Uncertainty-Aware Budget Allocation for Efficient LLM Tree Search

Jianguo Huang, Zhiyi Lyu, Fuxiang Zhang, Yanchen Deng, Deheng Ye, Bo An

Tree search enhances multi-step reasoning in large language models (LLMs), yet prevailing methods expand every step with a fixed budget, irrespective of the marginal utility of expansion. We first show, across existing search algorithms, that continuations sampled from a shared intermediate state are largely duplicates and collapse to a few distinct answers. Critically, we find that the semantic dispersion of sibling actions predicts the redundancy of their downstream continuations, permitting a subtree's budget to be fixed before expansion. An information-theoretic analysis motivates this: treating the per-step budget as the Monte Carlo sample size for estimating a step's residual answer entropy, we derive a sufficient budget that shrinks with sibling dispersion. We accordingly propose $\textbf{Uncertainty-Aware Allocation}$ (UAA), a training-free framework that decouples value from uncertainty: values determine which candidates are promising, while uncertainty, estimated from sibling dispersion at no additional model cost, determines how many merit expansion, via uncertainty-aware pruning and uncertainty-aware budgeting. Across mathematical reasoning, logical reasoning, and planning benchmarks, UAA reduces token usage by up to $90\%$ and latency by up to $9\times$ at comparable accuracy, generalizing across four tree search algorithms and both open- and closed-source LLMs.

Swati Gupta, Tianjiao Li

Yet LLM-assisted data curation creates a new operational tradeoff: higher-fidelity models and richer prompts can be substantially more expensive, while the value of this additional fidelity depends on the downstream optimization accuracy. This raises the central question of this work: when should one pay for a better LLM, given a specific downstream task at hand? We study this question through stochastic optimization with mixed-fidelity first-order oracles, which can have systematic bias and state-dependent variance. We develop two cost-aware algorithmic frameworks that allow adaptive switching between stochastic oracles. With known bias, the first meta-algorithm applies a restarted, cost-aware policy that adaptively selects oracle fidelity across optimization stages. With unknown bias, we develop a budgeted certification scheme that uses occasional trusted-gradient evaluations to certify the optimization progress and eliminate unsuccessful oracle sources, and show an explicit tradeoff between certification cost and delayed detection of biased sources. We provide concrete realizations of these meta-algorithms by applying biased stochastic gradient extrapolation (SGE) and recursive regularization under state-dependent noise as subroutines. Synthetic and LLM-completion experiments illustrate the qualitative switching behavior predicted by the theory: the adaptive policy skips several expensive LLM oracles and substantially reduces cumulative cost relative to fixed high-fidelity alternatives while achieving comparable accuracy.

When Does Momentum Help in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards?

Kaifei Wang, Zikang Shan, Liwei Wang, Yinyu Ye, Han Zhong

Momentum is a classical tool for accelerating convex optimization and a standard component of modern large language model training. Yet recent empirical work has questioned the usefulness of first-moment momentum in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). We study this question through a bandit formulation of RLVR with bias-corrected exponential moving average (EMA) momentum, where the EMA coefficient $β$ controls how quickly the weights on past gradients decay. When policy gradients are evaluated exactly, we prove that increasing $β$ within a range that prevents overshoot strictly accelerates optimization. When gradients are estimated from sampled responses, a positive expected gain can be obscured by sampling fluctuations. We show that as the learning rate $η$ decreases, the expected gain with fixed $β$ vanishes faster than these fluctuations, whereas increasing $β$ to keep $η/(1-β)$ within the safe regime preserves a nonvanishing gain that eventually dominates them. Bandit simulations quantitatively validate these predictions. RLVR experiments on Qwen3-4B-Base using SGD with EMA and AdamW support our principle of choosing larger coefficients in practical LLM post-training. On Qwen2.5-32B with AdamW, simply increasing $β_1$ improves mean validation accuracy by nearly $2$ percentage points over the conventional choice, demonstrating the principle's effectiveness at a larger model scale.

We illustrate Euler's capabilities through three mathematical research case studies and describe the implemented system and its software validation.

Learning Warm Starts for the Simplex Method from Measured Solver Cost

Felix Parker, Danielle A. Ripsman, Emmett Springer, Kimia Ghobadi

Learned warm starts for the simplex method build starting bases using predictions of which variables will be basic at optimality. Prior work has shown that starting bases predicted by graph neural networks can sharply reduce iteration counts, but the time saved may fall well short of the iterations saved. On 25 classes of linear programs (LPs), we find that a strong supervised basis predictor removes two thirds of the dual simplex's iterations but only a third of its time. Two effects explain the gap: the iterations removed by a good start tend to be cheap, and closeness to an optimal basis does not rank starts by cost. We prove that two starts with equal overlap with an optimal basis and equal pivot counts can differ in linear-algebra work by a factor that grows with the square root of the number of rows. Empirically, across the starts of one LP, the pivot count explains 71% of the variance of log solve time, while the CPU instruction count explains 98.5%. We introduce CostStart, which uses supervised prediction for the basis and trains a small policy on measured instruction counts to choose, per LP, how many predicted columns to use, which algorithm and pricing rule to run, and whether to warm-start at all. On unseen LPs of the training classes, including LPs four times the training size, CostStart roughly halves the solve time relative to the solver's default start and is 1.4 to 2.2 times as fast as two published methods retrained on the same data.

Gibbs-Gap Policy Optimization for Off-Policy RL with Verifiable Rewards

Xuanchang Zhang, Tong Zhang

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards often trains language models on responses generated by a different, possibly stale behavior policy. Correcting this mismatch with learner-to-behavior importance weights can introduce high variance for long responses. We introduce Gibbs-Gap Policy Optimization (GGPO), which minimizes a log-mean-exp contrast of KL-adjusted rewards among responses to the same prompt, without learner-to-behavior importance weights. We derive GGPO from KL-regularized policy optimization: on policy, its population loss is exactly the KL-regularized optimality gap, and under suitable coverage the learner's single-response expected reward is at least the behavior policy's best-of-$K$ reward, minus terms for the raw finite-group loss, regularization, and finite training data. We characterize reference refresh through exact Gibbs-target composition and a finite-data final-policy reward bound under local reference control and tokenwise behavior mismatch. This refresh is empirically important: in an ablation, fixed-reference GGPO scores $32.96$ on the five-benchmark problem-weighted average, versus $46.58$ with the default refresh schedule. Experiments on mathematical reasoning further show that GGPO outperforms GRPO and AGRO under training-inference mismatch and on fixed offline data, while maintaining its performance as controlled policy staleness grows.

Distributed Performance Boosting through Neural Control for Plug-and-Play Networks

Danilo Saccani, Leonardo Massai, Giancarlo Ferrari-Trecate

We address the problem of improving the performance of pre-stabilized networked systems subject to plug-and-play (PnP) operations, namely additions or removals of subsystems. While distributed baseline controllers can preserve stability after a PnP transition, augmenting them with expressive neural policies can invalidate the formal stability guarantees. To overcome this issue, we build on recently proposed Performance Boosting controllers based on the Internal Model Control (IMC) principle, which reduce closed-loop stability to a finite $\ell_2$-gain requirement on an auxiliary operator. In the distributed setting, this operator is formed by interconnecting local neural operators that exchange information only between neighboring subsystems. We then derive free parametrizations of this interconnection, namely explicit maps from unconstrained trainable variables to interconnected neural operators satisfying a prescribed network gain bound for every parameter value. The obtained distributed neural controller can therefore be trained by unconstrained gradient-based optimization without stability constraints or projection steps, while retaining the prescribed gain bound at every training iterate. We develop two complementary parametrizations. A neighbor-based compositional parametrization uses local coupling bounds, so that after a PnP event only subsystems whose bounds change need to update their gain parameters. A network-norm parametrization instead uses a bound on the overall coupling matrix and can reduce conservatism, at the price of requiring network-wide information. A PnP DC-microgrid case study shows that the proposed neural layer improves voltage transients under topology changes and load variations while preserving nominal closed-loop stability.
