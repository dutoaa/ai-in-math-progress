Rennala NSGD: Asynchronous Stochastic Optimization Beyond Euclidean Geometry

Igor Sokolov, Alexander Tyurin, Peter Richtárik

The empirical success of matrix-aware optimizers, such as Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) and Shampoo (arXiv:1802.09568), highlights the potential of non-Euclidean geometry for deep learning, including large language model (LLM) training. Despite this progress, the theoretical understanding of stochastic optimization in general normed spaces remains limited, particularly in distributed settings. We analyze minibatch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) for smooth nonconvex optimization with respect to an arbitrary norm on a finite-dimensional space. Using the framework of $κ$-regular normed spaces (arXiv:0809.0813), we derive high-probability oracle complexity bounds under a light-tail condition stated directly in the dual norm induced by the update norm. The bounds make the dependence on geometry explicit through the regularity constant $κ$ of the dual space. In the Euclidean case, our bound recovers the standard minibatch SGD rate up to logarithmic factors. For some non-Euclidean geometries and noise models, it is sharper than bounds obtained by converting a Euclidean noise assumption, reducing a linear dimension dependence to a logarithmic one. Building on this framework, we introduce Rennala NSGD, a semi-asynchronous distributed algorithm, and establish wall-clock time complexity guarantees under fixed, heterogeneous worker computation times. In the Euclidean case ($κ=1$), our bound matches the parameter dependence of classical Rennala SGD up to logarithmic factors.

Ramanujan Challenge Results: AI-Assisted Evaluation Pipeline for Multi-Format Proofs

Alan Sorani, Ashvni Narayanan, Michael Shalyt, Tali Monderer, Rotem Kalisch, Hila Barkan

As AI's mathematical capabilities rapidly improve, meaningful benchmarks are shifting from university-level problems and math olympiads to research-level mathematics. The Ramanujan Challenge is a benchmark comprised of research-level problems in number theory, including unpublished results and open conjectures. It presented concrete formulas for mathematical constants whose correctness can be checked numerically, while their proofs may require non-obvious mathematics. Here, we analyze the Challenge submissions, measuring the relative success of different AI models, Computer Algebra Systems (CAS), and formal tools. We find correct submissions that are conceptually distinct from the official solutions, and a proof of one of the published open conjectures. Evaluating the submissions exposed a second challenge: judging complex proofs reliably at scale. We introduce an AI-assisted evaluation pipeline for natural language proofs, CAS code, and Lean formalizations that assesses semantic and logical correctness. Compared with the conventional one-shot LLM judgment, our pipeline reduces false-positive verdicts on natural language proofs from 23% to 6% and improves judgment accuracy on supporting code from 46% to 58%. This approach provides a methodology for evaluating research-level mathematics at scale, with potential applications across mathematics and AI for science.

An Adaptive Transferable Neural Network for Partial Differential Equations with Anisotropic or Sharp Solutions

Jiacong Qu, Liyong Zhu

Transferable neural network (TransNet) has demonstrated great potential for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) with high accuracy and efficiency. However, it struggles significantly when dealing with PDEs featuring anisotropy or sharp solutions. In this work, we propose an adaptive transferable neural network (A-TransNet), a novel machine learning framework that adaptively expands neurons in regions with large solution gradients to effectively capture complex solution behaviors. Instead of relying on training or random sampling, the location parameters of newly added neurons are determined by integrating the active subspaces method and local gradient analysis based on posterior gradient information. This adaptive strategy enables neurons to be allocated where they are most needed, thereby improving the resolution of anisotropic and sharp features without additional computational costs. Moreover, the shape parameters of new neurons are automatically determined from the neuron density induced by their locations, further enhancing the network representation capability. An iterative neuron expansion strategy is developed, together with rigorous analysis of stability, a posteriori error estimation, and convergence of the proposed A-TransNet method. Extensive numerical experiments on challenging PDE problems with anisotropic and sharp solutions demonstrate that A-TransNet achieves superior accuracy, efficiency, and robustness compared with existing methods.

GTFormer: A Syntax-Free Graph Network Alternative to Transformers for 8192$\times$ Length Extrapolation in Linear Time

Elisabeth Fink

Self-attention mechanisms have established the Transformer as the dominant backbone across natural language processing, source code intelligence, and automated theorem proving. However, all-to-all pairwise token comparison enforces a quadratic $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ compute and memory barrier alongside severe softmax attention dilution as sequence length $N$ scales beyond the training horizon. Inspired by recent advances in algebraic graph learning (\textbf{WPNet}), this work introduces \textbf{GTFormer}, a syntax-free hierarchical Graph Neural Network (GNN) that replaces flat self-attention for general long-context sequence modeling. GTFormer organizes raw token streams into a bounded-degree hierarchy combining a balanced binary reduction tree ($\lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ depth), horizontal boundary-bridging shift edges, random expander shortcuts, multi-channel convex highway gating, and a top-down broadcast pass. Across eight long-context code, log, and multi-hop retrieval benchmarks trained exclusively on sequences of length $N \le 512$, GTFormer extrapolates zero-shot to $N = 4{,}194{,}304$ ($8192\times$) on a single commodity GPU in linear $\mathcal{O}(N)$ memory, maintaining near-perfect accuracy where standard and RoPE Transformers suffer from out-of-distribution attention collapse and out-of-memory exhaustion.

FARMS: A Verifiable Foundation for Automated Computational Science

Di Wu, Ling Liang, Haizhao Yang

Automated computational science could accelerate discovery by selecting, adapting and developing numerical methods. This prospect requires mathematical knowledge that AI systems can interpret and reuse with explicit guarantees. Here we present FARMS, a faithful and efficient foundation that transforms computational mathematics into AI-ready computing knowledge. FARMS connects formal statements to source context, algorithmic meaning and quantitative guarantees through source preparation, reusable representations and semantic review. FARMS therefore supports an affordable, reusable knowledge foundation for trustworthy automated computational science.

DecompGym: Benchmarking LLM Agents on Designing Decomposition Algorithms

Sikai Cheng, Tinghan Ye, Arnaud Deza, Xiaonan Qi, Miao Li, Litong Liu

Benders decomposition and column generation solve large optimization problems by passing cuts, columns, and dual values between a master problem and subproblems, and are hard to implement correctly. Large language models now automate parts of algorithm design, which points toward agents that build decomposition solvers for new problems. As a first step toward such agents, we study how well current coding agents design and implement these two algorithms, and how they fail. We introduce DecompGym, 81 instances from nine problem families (five Benders, four column generation), in which an agent writes a solver for a prescribed method. Each solver must meet three criteria: execution within a 60-second time limit; numerical feasibility together with a method audit, in which an LLM judge reads the solver's code; and an objective within 1% of the stored optimal objective. We evaluate 12 GPT, Claude, and Gemini models running in coding-agent tools. Over up to ten attempts, we compare independent regeneration with reflection, which first diagnoses the failure. On the first attempt, two models solve every instance and seven solve more than 90%. Most Benders failures (48 of 62) are missing or suboptimal solutions. The most common column-generation failure is a method-audit rejection. Output checks alone would accept 93 of the 108 attempts that fail the method audit. The most common rejection, an optimalif, occurs 96 times, 92 of them in one model.Reflection turns 66% of retries into successes, compared with 39% for regeneration, and it repairs method-audit failures, for which the judge names the defecective gaps. Agents for decomposition therefore need checks on how a solver reaches its answer and feedback that locates the error. The benchmark is available at https://github.com/SIKAI-C/DecompGym.

LLM-Assisted Optimization for Simulation-Based Redesign

Chan-Eui Song, Jeremy J. Michalek, Christopher McComb

Engineering design frequently involves redesigning a prior solution to adapt to changes in context, such as material prices or system requirements. Redesign often leverages simulators too complex and/or computationally intensive for traditional numerical solvers, which lack engineering knowledge and context, to ensure global solution quality. Large language models (LLMs) can interpret semantic information, including engineering knowledge about the system and changes in context, to help guide search, and recent studies have shown that incorporating this knowledge into optimization can improve search performance. However, how best to combine LLMs with numerical solvers in redesign remains unclear. We develop and test an integrated LLM-optimization approach for redesign and compare three methods for leveraging LLMs (warm start, variable fixing and ReAct) and four black-box solvers (Bayesian optimization, genetic algorithms, mesh adaptive direct search and random search) on an electric vehicle battery pack cost minimization case study across four types of redesign scenarios. We find that the integrated approach can match feasible design discovery of the numerical-solver baselines with 80% fewer simulator evaluations while tripling the fraction of runs that find near-optimal solutions. We find that the best LLM-solver integration approach and the way the prior solution is leveraged depend on the structure of the design requirement change, and that the benefit of increasing LLM model size grows with the extent to which the LLM controls the search. These results show clear potential for LLMs in redesign tasks for guiding or replacing numerical solvers.

Exploring AI Peer in Collaborative Mathematics Learning

Yiran Lin, Ye Deng, Ziqi Pan, Yongan Yu, Alexandra I. Cristea

Artificial intelligence (AI) agents are increasingly explored as collaborative learning partners in mathematics education, yet it remains unclear what makes an AI recognizable as a peer. This study examines how domain experts perceive and interact with an adaptive LLM-based peer agent combining a Big Five-informed persona with motivational state modulation for collaborative mathematics problem solving. Through thematic analysis of expert interviews, we identify conditions shaping peer perceptions, including emotional safety, shared control, and collaborative reasoning. Participants primarily judged the AI through its social behavior rather than mathematical capabilities. The AI was perceived more as a peer when it created a non-judgmental space, engaged in tentative reasoning, displayed appropriate imperfection, and respected participants' pace. Conversely, blind agreement and over-instruction reduced trust and shifted perceptions toward a tutor role. Based on these findings, we propose human-centered design dimensions for AI peer agents to guide future peer-oriented learning systems.

H$^2$R: Hierarchical Hint-Based Reward for Mathematical Reasoning via Reinforcement Learning

He Tu, Siye Li, Hongyi Wen

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards has shown great promise in enhancing mathematical reasoning in large language models. However, existing approaches provide limited intermediate supervision for complex, multi-step reasoning tasks. To bridge this gap, we propose \textbf{Hierarchical Hint-based Reward}, a framework that leverages the intrinsic sub-question structure of mathematical problems as structured intermediate conditioning signals during RLVR training. Unlike chain-of-thought prompting, these hierarchical hints serve as logically grounded intermediate milestones that effectively reduce the reasoning horizon toward the final answer. To validate this framework, we introduce \textbf{HiMath}, a benchmark of hierarchically structured mathematical problems. Across diverse LLMs, hierarchical hints consistently improve reasoning performance even without task-specific training. When integrated into RL training via H$^2$R, they boost Qwen2.5-Math-7B accuracy on HiMath from 30.4\% to 39.1\% using hierarchical hints, and up to 45.6\% with Oracle Hierarchical Hints. This pronounced improvement demonstrates that incorporating reliable intermediate checkpoints into reward design is essential for sustaining complex mathematical reasoning.

OpenFrontiers: A Collection of Open Research Problems and a Benchmark for Scientific Rediscovery

Jiarui Yao, Ruida Wang, Tong Zhang

Large language models (LLMs) have shown increasing promise in research-level problem solving, yet a comprehensive collection of such problems is still missing, and evaluating them on research-level problems remains challenging. We introduce OpenFrontiers, a cross-disciplinary resource that combines a structured corpus of open research problems with a retrospective benchmark of recently resolved problems in mathematics. The corpus records problem statements, context, progress, and other information for 12,151 problems. The benchmark contains 200 problems, 110 resolved in 2026 and 90 still open, each with a Lean~4 statement checked by automated probes and two independent reviews; a subset HQ-50 further filters based on quality score, etc. Our evaluation protocol grades formal proofs with two independent checkers in an isolated sandbox under closed-book, frozen-retrieval, and live-web conditions. Evaluating seven LLM agents on the HQ-50 subset under a standardized budget, we find that closed-book rediscovery is rare, at most 7 of 40 resolved problems, whereas live-web success mostly reflects retrieval of published solutions. By linking a broad research-problem corpus to a formally graded evaluation subset with documented solutions, OpenFrontiers provides a foundation for measuring research-level rediscovery and studying progress toward solving currently open scientific problems. The dataset and benchmark could be found in https://huggingface.co/collections/FlippyDora/openfrontiers.

Neural Network Approximation and Structural Complexity of Semilinear PDEs with Lower Bounds

Hayden Schaeffer

Neural networks are commonly used to learn the solution operators of PDEs such as the Navier-Stokes equations and viscous conservation laws. On standard benchmarks, moderately sized and often shallow networks achieve small errors that decay at algebraic rates with the network size. These observations are not well described by the current approximation theory, which treats the solution operator as a generic map and derives worst-case bounds over large classes of operators. We show that the structure of the equations and their properties explain the observed behavior. For semilinear parabolic equations, the approximation costs separate into two terms, where one is controlled by an invariant of the governing law and the other by the data. The constitutive law can be parameterized as a single block whose size for accuracy $η$ is $O(η^{-pχ}\logη^{-1})$ for every $p>p_f$, where the structural exponent $p_f$ only depends on the computation graph of $f$. This exponent does not depend on the dimension and is in fact zero for polynomial laws, so for the two-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations the nonlinear block costs only $O(\logη^{-1})$ parameters. The data enters through encoding layers with a bounded number of learned weights per mode, so neither term grows with the grid resolution or the time step. While the analysis only uses the max-norm error of the pointwise block, we show that one obtains approximation bounds in a stronger norm along the whole trajectory. For viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equations with a $C^s$ Hamiltonian, the approximation requires on the order of $η^{-1/s}$ parameters, and we prove a matching lower bound, up to logarithms. This shows that solution operators for this class of problems have low structural complexity, even though they are nonlinear maps between infinite-dimensional spaces.

Data-driven Random-feature Approximation of Koopman Generators of Nonlinear Continuous-time Systems

Ana Čukarska, Maximilian Gollwitzer, Viola Muning Li, Vladyslav Fediukov, Zahra Monfared, Felix Dietrich

The Koopman formalism is often used here, because it is a flexible approach that is informative about the system's fundamental properties. The formalism represents dynamical systems through linear operators acting on a suitable function space. A practical difficulty arising in the numerical approximation of Koopman operators is the choice of a ``dictionary'', meaning an effective finite-dimensional representation of the function space. In this paper, we focus on learning dynamical systems in continuous-time, and so we consider numerical approximations of the Koopman generator instead of the Koopman operator. Our main contribution is a data-driven random-feature method to obtain a parsimonious and effective function representation for Galerkin approximation methods such as gEDMD. The representation we construct is data-informed, which allows us to improve upon data-agnostic methods. We empirically demonstrate the suitability of our method for forecasting tasks and system analysis. The numerical experiments show that our approach is accurate, and faster than iterative optimization. Further, we prove convergence of our approximation of the vector field and the resulting trajectories. In addition to the numerical results, we also make theoretical contributions, including a general convergence criterion for the semigroup generated by the gEDMD approximation and a counterexample showing that gEDMD does not generally preserve exponential boundedness. With our contribution, we advance the frontier of efficiently learning continuous-time dynamics from data, towards interpretable and mathematically robust modelling based on the Koopman framework.

Deciding two-variable logic with global and local Presburger quantification

Michael Benedikt, Chia-Hsuan Lu, Alex Perry, Tony Tan

We look at a two-variable logic over finite graphs that allows Presburger quantifiers: linear arithmetic inequalities between the number of elements that satisfy subformulas. The prior literature shows that closing two-variable logic under Presburger quantification leads to undecidability, while closing under \emph{local quantification} -- the counts are always over neighbors of a given node -- preserves decidability. Here we show decidability of satisfiability for an intermediate logic that allows both local counts and global counts, but where the linear constraints do not mix the two. The proof requires a significant extension of the prior machinery developed for two-variable logic with counting. As a corollary we obtain decidability of satisfiability for an important family of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). GNNs are a popular graph learning formalism. For example, in a variation of GNNs considered in the past literature, local aggregation is used in each layer to create modified values at each vertex of a graph, and then global aggregation is used in a final layer to obtain a single numerical value for the graph as a whole. Our logic is motivated by the fact that mixing of local counting and global aggregation in GNNs has been shown to lead to undecidability. Our results here show that if global and local aggregation are separated, one regains decidability. Decidability of other static analysis problems, like equivalence of GNNs, follows from the decidability of satisfiability.

Conic-Lifting for Learning-rate-free Convex Optimization

Julien Grand-Clément, Joon Kwon, Francesco Orabona

A central topic in online and stochastic optimization is to design algorithms that do not require tuning of hyperparameters. Successful strategies in online learning include AdaGrad-style learning rates, Conic Blackwell algorithms, and parameter-free algorithms. However, these classes require different, often difficult, analyses. We develop a unifying family of Online Convex Optimization (OCO) algorithms that require no user-tuned learning rate on convex domains that may be unbounded. The construction lifts the domain to a cone by adding one scalar coordinate, an idea going back to Blackwell (1954). The lifted subgradients are orthogonal to the corresponding lifted decisions, leaving the comparator scale free to optimize in the regret analysis. Applying Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) and Online Mirror Descent (OMD) on the cone recovers Conic Blackwell Algorithm (CBA) and Conic Blackwell Algorithm Plus (CBA$^+$) on bounded domains in Hilbert spaces and extends them to finite-dimensional normed spaces with a strongly convex squared norm. For arbitrary nonempty closed convex domains in these spaces, a known gradient bound yields parameter-free regret guarantees. Surprisingly, on an unconstrained Hilbert space, the construction recovers the Krichevsky--Trofimov (KT) coin-betting algorithm exactly. We also give a parameter-free algorithm for unconstrained domains that requires no gradient bound.

Data-driven Regularization for Inverse Problems

Oleh Melnyk, Sebastian Neumayer

In many applications, we are interested in studying an object of interest $x$. However, its properties are often not directly measurable. Instead, we only observe $y = F(x)$, where the (known) function $F$ is referred to as the forward mapping. To infer about $x$ from the observation $y$, we seek an (approximate) inverse mapping $x \approx F^{-1}(y)$. This process is known as solving the inverse problem. In practice, the inverse mapping $F^{-1}$ may be ill-defined or discontinuous, which forces us to choose a certain solution from many possible ones based on prior assumptions on $x$. Such assumptions about $x$ are typically incorporated into the inversion process through regularization. Therefore, the first part of this course focuses on the classical regularization theory of inverse problems. In the second part, we transition to algorithmic approaches for solving inverse problems. The foundation for this lies in the variational formulation of inverse problems, where reconstructions are obtained by minimizing specific functionals. Here, we will explore key optimization principles, discuss their practical implementation, and examine how they contribute to robust reconstructions. Today, classical methods are often modified to incorporate data-driven machine learning techniques, which improve the empirical performance. However, this often comes at the cost of explainability or convergence guarantees. Such properties are critical, for example, in medical applications, where one wants to be confident that reconstructed abnormalities are of physical nature and not phantoms resulting from the method. For these reasons, we discuss a number of machine learning methods for inverse problems, such as unrolling techniques, learned regularizers and plug-and-play methods, and explore their performance guarantees.

Optimization-based lane-changing trajectory planning in mixed traffic with embedded input convex neural networks

Yuanxiang Yang, Yu Liu, Fabricio Oliveira, Yu Han, Claudio Roncoli

Lane-changing (LC) maneuvers are highly challenging vehicle interactions, with substantial implications for traffic safety and efficiency. Developing effective LC strategies for connected and automated vehicles in mixed traffic is complicated by the uncertainty associated with surrounding-vehicle behavior and the computational burden of embedding realistic prediction models within optimization schemes. To capture these complex interactions while retaining tractability, this study proposes a data-driven optimization framework that embeds neural network (NN) surrogates for safety and lateral comfort within a Bézier-curve-based trajectory planner. Specifically, the safety cost during the LC process is quantified via a surrogate composed of an input convex recurrent neural network (ICRNN) encoding the planned trajectory, a fully input convex neural network (FICNN) embedding the LC duration, and a partially input convex neural network (PICNN) decoder fusing them with historical traffic states; separately, another FICNN surrogate approximates the optimal lateral comfort cost as a function of the LC duration. Both surrogates are embedded via linear programming (LP) formulations, avoiding the binary variables required by conventional mixed-integer programming (MIP) approaches for NN-embedded optimization. We further theoretically establish their exactness at optimality and extend the framework to problems with additional analytical objective terms and multiple surrogates. Experiments on a hybrid dataset combining real-world and simulated trajectories demonstrate that the proposed method achieves surrogate accuracy competitive with unconstrained baselines while substantially improving optimization efficiency over conventional MIPs. Closed-loop simulations further show that the proposed planners markedly reduce the weighted planning cost and consistently outperform baseline methods.

Distributionally robust chance-constrained standard quadratic optimization with Wasserstein ambiguity

Immanuel M. Bomze, Daniel De Vicente, Simge Küçükyavuz

The standard quadratic optimization problem consists of minimizing a quadratic form over the standard simplex. It has highly relevant applications ranging from portfolio optimization to machine learning. However, if the quadratic form is neither convex nor concave, this problem is NP-hard. We study a distributionally robust chance-constrained variant whose ambiguity set is a Wasserstein ball around the sampled distribution and make four contributions. First, although the problem is nonconvex in the decision variable, we exploit its linear dependence on the uncertain matrix to reduce the problem to a mixed-integer quadratic optimization problem. Second, we show that a judicious choice of matrix norm---the entrywise maximum norm---makes this reformulation both exact and tractable, and we describe the induced worst-case distribution. Third, our formulation is, by construction, the minimization of the worst-case Value-at-Risk of the random quadratic form; this unifies it with existing frameworks for uncertain standard quadratic optimization problems, in particular under chance constraints, and we identify natural settings in which it collapses to a single deterministic instance. Finally, we report numerical experiments on positive-definite and indefinite instances which quantify how the model parameters drive the optimal value and solution time, and expose the computational cost of indefiniteness.

Claims of general intelligence require evidence that models can transfer learnt principles to areas with few worked examples.

A Physics-Informed Neural Network for Geodesic Computation and Visualization of Geodesic Spheres on Riemannian Manifolds

Sujit Bhattacharyya

Visualizing geodesic structures associated with general Riemannian metrics is a challenging problem in computational differential geometry. In this work, we present a computational framework for visualizing geodesics and geodesic spheres on a three-dimensional Riemannian manifold equipped with the metric $g=\text{diag}(1,x^2,1), x>0$. The associated Christoffel symbols and curvature tensors are computed symbolically, and the resulting geodesic equations are solved using both \texttt{SciPy}'s numerical integration method and a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN). The two solutions are compared to assess the accuracy of the neural network approximation. We further develop geodesic spike diagrams and surface visualizations to illustrate the deformation of geodesic spheres relative to their Euclidean counterparts. Intrinsic Riemannian surface area and volume are estimated numerically using the induced metric and the Riemannian volume density, respectively. For the chosen metric and a geodesic ball of radius one, the computed area and volume are approximately $0.362\%$ and $0.364\%$ smaller than the corresponding Euclidean values at the base grid resolution. A grid-refinement study is also performed to examine the sensitivity of these estimates to numerical resolution. The proposed framework demonstrates how symbolic differential geometry, numerical integration, and PINNs can be combined to investigate and visualize geometric structures associated with Riemannian metrics.
