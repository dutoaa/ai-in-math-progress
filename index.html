GTFormer: A Syntax-Free Graph Network Alternative to Transformers for 8192$\times$ Length Extrapolation in Linear Time

Elisabeth Fink

Self-attention mechanisms have established the Transformer as the dominant backbone across natural language processing, source code intelligence, and automated theorem proving. However, all-to-all pairwise token comparison enforces a quadratic $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ compute and memory barrier alongside severe softmax attention dilution as sequence length $N$ scales beyond the training horizon. Inspired by recent advances in algebraic graph learning (\textbf{WPNet}), this work introduces \textbf{GTFormer}, a syntax-free hierarchical Graph Neural Network (GNN) that replaces flat self-attention for general long-context sequence modeling. GTFormer organizes raw token streams into a bounded-degree hierarchy combining a balanced binary reduction tree ($\lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ depth), horizontal boundary-bridging shift edges, random expander shortcuts, multi-channel convex highway gating, and a top-down broadcast pass. Across eight long-context code, log, and multi-hop retrieval benchmarks trained exclusively on sequences of length $N \le 512$, GTFormer extrapolates zero-shot to $N = 4{,}194{,}304$ ($8192\times$) on a single commodity GPU in linear $\mathcal{O}(N)$ memory, maintaining near-perfect accuracy where standard and RoPE Transformers suffer from out-of-distribution attention collapse and out-of-memory exhaustion.

NextEval: An Agent-Based Solver and Benchmark for Derivative-Free Optimization

Shuo Chen, Li Cui, Cunxin Huang, Ya-Feng Liu, Minghang Xie, Meng Xu

Derivative-free optimization (DFO) addresses problems where derivatives are unavailable or costly to obtain. This setting often arises in simulation-based and experimental applications, where evaluating the black-box objective can itself be costly. Thus, it remains a central challenge in DFO to effectively exploit accumulated observations to select informative evaluation points under a limited budget. In this light, we introduce the NextEval solver, an agent-based DFO solver, which integrates the capabilities of a large language model (LLM) to guide the sequential selection of evaluation points. Specifically, we establish a system that combines an LLM agent with a numerical environment. The environment provides tools for analyzing past evaluations and executing numerical methods, while maintaining a shared evaluation history and method-specific state to support continuation across method switches. Using observations and tool feedback, the LLM agent selects tools, configures their parameters, and allocates the evaluation budget, while numerical methods compute and evaluate candidate points. Building on this system, we also introduce NextEval Bench, a benchmark protocol for comparing agent configurations on shared DFO tasks using the same numerical tools under matched evaluation budgets and information constraints. Numerical results on low-dimensional DFO problems demonstrate that the NextEval solver outperforms the state-of-the-art DFO baselines in function-evaluation efficiency.

Improving Reasoning with Rollout Agreement for Group-Based Policy Optimization

Sohyun Son, Yerim Hong, Jaeyun Park, Mujeen Sung

Recent methods for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) use rollout diversity to improve reasoning performance or training efficiency. Despite these gains, the extent to which differences among correct-answer rollouts reflect useful alternative reasoning or unresolved errors remains poorly understood. We investigate this question through a blind paired analysis of a model trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), comparing the highest- and lowest-agreement correct-answer traces for the same problem. Across 126 pairs, low-agreement traces have more spurious reasoning than high-agreement traces (35% vs. 22%), including internal inconsistencies and misuse of problem conditions. Based on these findings, we propose RoAR (Rollout-Agreement Reward), which adds reasoning-level agreement to GRPO's rewards for verified-correct rollouts. RoAR computes this signal from existing on-policy rollout groups, requiring no human-written rationales, additional sampling, or separately trained process evaluator. On five mathematical reasoning benchmarks, the 1.5B GRPO-RoAR model reaches 55.5% mean pass@1 under zero-shot evaluation using 7K training examples, exceeding GRPO and GRPO-CARE by 6.0 and 5.5 percentage points. A separate blind audit of 100 problems answered correctly by all four 1.5B models finds spurious rates of 18% for GRPO-RoAR, 32% for GRPO, 27% for GRPO-CARE, and 37% for DRA-GRPO. Our findings identify reasoning validity as an important consideration when using rollout differences to guide learning. Our code is available at https://github.com/ldi-kyunghee/RoAR.

Log-Derivative Trick for Parametric Markov Chains

Arthur Gall, Ichiro Hasuo, Thomas Seiller

Optimizing expected rewards and reachability probabilities in parametric Markov chains (pMCs) is central to the analysis and synthesis of probabilistic systems with loops. While traditional symbolic synthesis approaches--such as parameter lifting and rational function generation--suffer from scalability bottlenecks, gradient-based optimization emerges as an effective alternative. However, existing gradient-based techniques still rely on symbolic gradient computation--such as solving large linear systems--over the global state space, inheriting scalability limits. Finite-horizon approximation is possible but introduces uncontrollable bias. In this paper, we introduce a scalable Monte Carlo gradient estimator based on a measure-theoretic generalization of the log-derivative trick. We prove that our estimator provides unbiased gradient estimates under almost-sure termination and standard regularity conditions. Crucially, our method avoids global state-space materialization and linear system solving entirely: it operates purely along sampled trajectories, requiring only black-box evaluations of local transition probabilities (or densities) and their derivatives. Unlike existing pMC techniques that are fundamentally restricted to finite discrete systems, our measure-theoretic framework naturally extends to continuous and general $σ$-finite state spaces. Empirical evaluations on synthetic discrete pMCs and continuous-state case studies demonstrate that our method scales to state spaces far beyond the reach of existing solvers, significantly reducing execution time without introducing bias.

ADACT: PDE-Constrained Thermal Hardware/Software Co-Design Adjoint-Driven Optimal Control via Finite Difference Discretization

Don Li

By virtue of the sheer compute demands of large-scale artificial intelligence (AI) and high-performance computing (HPC), modern multi-core microprocessors suffer from severe power density limits and thermal throttling, entailing an urgent need for principled methods for predictive, physics-informed dynamical thermal management (DTM). Here, we introduce a hardware/software co-design physics-informed PDE-constrained DTM throttling optimal control algorithm, ADACT (Adjoint-Driven Adaptive Co-Design Thermal Control), that minimizes thermal risk and cooling energy overhead while maximizing core execution throughput. The underlying physical temperature field is governed by a two-dimensional nonhomogeneous parabolic reaction-diffusion equation with spatially localized, time-varying power source terms and variable convective cooling subject to nonhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. We employ a discretization of the continuous state PDE via a Crank-Nicolson finite difference scheme with a 5-point discrete Laplacian operator on a uniform spatial mesh, which preserves unconditional numerical stability and $O(Δx^2 + Δy^2 + Δt^2)$ second-order temporal and spatial accuracy. To efficiently compute exact gradients, we construct and implement a continuous backward-in-time adjoint state method with L-BFGS as our optimization algorithm of choice. The accuracy and second-order convergence rates of both forward state and backward adjoint solvers are quantitatively verified via the Method of Manufactured Solutions (MMS), and gradient accuracy is validated using directional derivative Taylor tests. Numerical experiments demonstrate that ADACT dynamically throttles software power workloads and modulates hardware cooling efforts to strictly uphold physical thermal thresholds while maintaining operational throughput.

Sparse Sum-of-Squares Layers for Certified Learning

Ege Gursoy, Nicolas Mansard

A central limitation of modern machine learning systems is the lack of formal certificates guaranteeing their behavior beyond empirical, sample-based evaluation. Sum-of-squares (SOS) methods provide such guarantees by certifying polynomial inequalities over continuous domains via semidefinite programming (SDP). While prior work has introduced SDP layers as differentiable components in neural networks, repeated learning calls can lose the computational benefit of the forward program's sparse representation. We propose Sparse SOS Layers, differentiable value layers whose backward passes reuse the local coordinates of the forward solve. Under a fixed template and learned objective coefficients, each layer avoids building dense certificates and reuses its sparse structure across training steps. Compared with existing methods, our approach achieves up to 2.7 times speedup in total warm-call time, with nearly 100 times lower memory usage (1.7 vs. 160 GiB) for relatively large problem sizes for the tested dense parameterization. Experiments cover sparse polynomial certification, pendulum control, combinatorial relaxations, robotics projection, oscillator chains, and pose graph synchronization. A design study achieves zero certified violations across unseen problem instances and sizes, while requiring roughly 7 times fewer optimization solves than estimating gradients by perturbing the objective coefficients and re-solving the problem (finite differences). These results show that sparse certificate values can be effectively integrated into learning pipelines when the backward pass respects the verifier's structure.

Balanced Active Subspaces for Optimal Control via Trajectory Optimization

Emmanuel S. Ameh, Samuel E. Otto

The curse of dimensionality associated with solving the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation for the value function remains a key obstacle for real-time optimal feedback control of high-dimensional nonlinear dynamical systems. This is especially severe in aerospace applications such as relaminarizing unsteady turbulent flows and recovering from aerodynamic stall where nonlinear dynamics play a significant role. We introduce Balanced Active Subspaces for Optimal Control (BASOC), a model reduction framework which seeks to find directions in state space along which the value function is most sensitive, and the distribution of states exhibits maximum variance along optimal trajectories. BASOC leverages trajectory optimization and the Pontryagin minimum principle to obtain state and co-state (value function gradient) data along locally optimal trajectories for constructing state and gradient covariance matrices. Balancing these matrices provides an oblique projection for approximating the value function, which can also be used to construct reduced-order models and surrogate feedback control laws. We show that for linear-quadratic optimal control problems, BASOC becomes equivalent to a snapshot method for initial condition balancing of the optimally controlled closed-loop realization. The framework is demonstrated on a nonlinear three-dimensional system where POD and balanced truncation are known to perform poorly, and on the nonlinear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation to demonstrate feasibility for larger-scale systems.

The Geometry of Manifold Learning: Singular foliations via Deep Learning models

Gabriele Novelli, Eliot Tron, Emanuele Latini, Rita Fioresi

Deep learning algorithms have become widely used and successful across a wide range of tasks. A key challenge is determining whether these algorithms truly capture the intrinsic geometry of their training data in order to better understand what they learn from data. In this theoretical work, we explore a new geometric structure defined on the data space by a trained neural network through the Data Information Matrix (DIM), a variation of the Fisher Information Matrix. This new geometric structure is a foliation and the main focus of this work is to explore the non-smooth case of ReLU activation functions, where traditional geometric tools often fail. The foliation is defined through a distribution spanned by the columns of the DIM. We first prove that this distribution is non-smooth in the sense of distributions, rendering the existence of an associated foliation much more complex. To circumvent this issue, we introduce a smoothed version of this distribution that differs from the original only on a set of measure zero. Finally, we prove that this smoothed distribution induces a singular foliation on the data space, which is based on the data the network has been trained with. Our theoretical contributions provide a geometric framework to better understand how neural networks organize their input space in non-smooth settings, bridging differential geometry and deep learning theory.

Wasserstein approximation of measures via orthogonal frames: statistical and computational complexities

Mateo Díaz, Nicolás García Trillos, Daniel López-Castaño, Congwei Yang

Dimension-reduction methods such as PCA identify a subspace capturing most of the variation in the data, but not a distinguished basis within it. Selecting a basis in which the samples are sparsely represented is a recurring task in unsupervised learning, addressed by methods in factor analysis, dictionary learning, and subspace clustering. We pose this task as Wasserstein approximation of the data distribution by measures supported on the axes of an orthogonal frame, which, in turn, reduces to a nonsmooth optimization problem over the orthogonal group $\mathrm{O}(d)$. We show that, under mild assumptions, both empirical risk minimizers and a locally initialized stochastic gradient method recover the population-optimal frame at the near-parametric rate $\widetilde{O}_d(n^{-1/2})$, where $n$ is the sample size. The main difficulty is that, although the population objective is smooth, the empirical objective is not-this mismatch precludes standard covering arguments. We overcome this issue by replacing Lipschitz continuity of the empirical gradient with a coarsely Lipschitz bound, a tool that may be of independent interest. Numerical experiments support our theoretical findings.

Intrinsic Gradient Barriers in Pushforward Deep Generative Models

Edric Tam, Qingyang Liu, Barbara Engelhardt, Ray Bai

Broad classes of deep generative models (DGMs) operate by learning to map a simple source distribution, such as a standard Gaussian, to a complex high-dimensional target distribution. Despite their success, DGMs often empirically struggle to accurately represent distributions with heavy tails or well-separated modes. In this work, we show that these difficulties reflect intrinsic properties of the underlying transport problem rather than particular neural network architectures or training procedures. We establish quantitative lower bounds on the gradients and Jacobians of pushforward mappings that transport log-concave source distributions to such targets. For heavy-tailed target distributions, the required gradient magnitude grows as the target develops increasingly large moments. For multimodal target distributions, the low-probability regions between well-separated modes similarly force large gradients in the transport map. We discuss how such large derivatives relate to trade-offs between expressivity and numerical stability. Our results hold under substantially weaker regularity conditions than previous analyses and remain stable under perturbations of the target distribution. Finally, we show that these obstructions can be mitigated when the goal is approximation of a given density rather than generative modeling. Specifically, we propose simple classes of bijective reparametrizations that can alleviate the difficult geometric structure and reduce the gradient burden placed on the learned generative map. Numerical experiments support our theoretical analysis.

Understanding Practical Optimization in Machine Learning

Egor Shulgin

Modern machine learning relies on practical optimization methods that often differ from the algorithms studied in classical analyses: they train submodels, clip or normalize gradients, use layer-wise non-Euclidean directions, or approximate costly inner computations. This thesis analyzes the objective, assumptions, update, and computation defined by these procedures, rather than those of simplified proxies. For submodel training, we show that Independent Subnetwork Training can converge to a shifted fixed point because sketching before differentiation biases the update for the original objective. Model-Agnostic Sparsified Training instead incorporates the sketch distribution into its objective, which makes sketched gradients unbiased and admits convergence guarantees. For private optimization, we show that adaptive quantile clipping retains bias at any fixed quantile, while suitable quantile schedules recover near-optimal nonconvex rates under heavy-tailed noise. We then combine smoothed normalization with error compensation and obtain distributed private convergence guarantees under standard assumptions, without assuming bounded gradients. For optimizers based on linear minimization oracles, we develop and validate a layer-wise generalized smoothness model and build on it the Gluon framework for Muon- and Scion-style methods. We also show that the approximate orthogonalization in Muon matters: less precise orthogonalization calls for smaller stepsizes, so its precision should be tuned together with the learning rate. Overall, practical methods are better understood when theory retains the features that determine their optimization behavior.
