A Convergence Analysis of the Random Feature Method for Linear PDEs

Jingrun Chen, Yixin Luo, Xiaojun Peng

The Random Feature Method (RFM) distinguishes itself among machine learning approaches to partial differential equations (PDEs) through its superior accuracy and computational efficiency. Nevertheless, the theoretical underpinnings of RFM remain incompletely elucidated. This paper provides a comprehensive analysis of RFM within the framework of kernel methods, focusing on two principal aspects: (i) approximation by finitely many random features in Sobolev norms and the PDE residual seminorm, and (ii) estimation over the induced reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) from finitely many noiseless collocation observations. We relate both problems to kernel ridge regression (KRR) and establish upper and lower bounds in terms of the eigenvalues of the associated integral operators under the respective assumptions. Combining the approximation and estimation lower bounds, we establish a lower bound under simultaneous restrictions on the numbers of random features and collocation observations. We further quantify how activation regularity affects these eigenvalues, obtaining algebraic upper bounds for finitely smooth activations and stretched-exponential upper bounds for analytic activations.

Its wo-scale architecture integrates theorem formalization and proof formalization agents, empowered by structured proof decomposition, parallel verification, accumulated proof experience, and selective semantic review. Notably, Turing Agent, built on DeepSeek, successfully completes the formal verification of three previously unresolved TCS tasks from OpenAI's October 2026 collection. One particularly challenging TCS problem required about a hundred formally verified auxiliary claims. Moreover, by actively exploring and refining the formalization process, Turing Agent can uncover simpler alternative proofs, expose unnecessary dependencies in original proofs, and generate reusable mathematical knowledge as a byproduct of verification.

Rethinking Supervision Scaling in On-Policy Distillation: From Dense Supervision to Stochastic Horizons

Xinhao Yao, Zhenxing Dou, Yong Liu

On-policy distillation (OPD) provides dense token-level supervision along student-generated trajectories, yet the learning benefits of longer trajectories may not scale proportionally with their computational cost. We investigate how supervision utility evolves with trajectory length. Controlled experiments reveal diminishing returns from additional supervised tokens, while showing that later positions remain learnable and exhibit substantial cross-position supervision transfer. Gradient analysis further identifies shared update components across response positions and diverse position-specific residuals whose cross-rollout consistency decreases at greater depths. Motivated by these findings, we propose Stochastic Horizons On-Policy Distillation (SH-OPD), which reduces redundant supervision by randomly varying rollout horizons while preserving access to deeper response states. SH-OPD samples a shared horizon for each training batch, enabling efficient parallel generation without per-sequence horizon scheduling overhead. We characterize the resulting reweighting bias and derive a compute-reweighting trade-off under standard optimization assumptions. Experiments on mathematical reasoning and code generation demonstrate that SH-OPD achieves slightly higher mathematical reasoning accuracy at approximately 45% of the per-update cost of standard OPD, while maintaining competitive code generation performance at around 60% of the baseline cost. These results highlight the benefits of efficient supervision allocation across response depths.

Second scaling regime in high-dimensional function approximation and curse of dimensionality

Shukai Du, Thu Le, Samuel N. Stechmann

High-dimensional function approximation is often subject to the curse of dimensionality, commonly described by a hypercube scaling regime with a large expense of $R^d$ grid points, where $R$ denotes the side length of a hypercube in $d$ dimensions. Here we show that a second scaling regime, which we call the lattice ball scaling regime, also arises and is relevant for practical applications. In this regime, the expense scales as $d^{R^2}/(R^2!)$ and therefore grows polynomially with $d$. This regime arises without additional special assumptions of special types of function spaces or error norms. The lattice ball scaling regime occurs when $R^2\ll d$, due to the discrete nature of counting the expense as the number of lattice points inside a hypersphere. Here we investigate this regime, which arises from the classical problem of approximating high-dimensional functions from common spaces of analytic or $m$-times continuously differentiable functions using orthogonal series (spectral method) such as Fourier, Chebyshev, and Legendre polynomial series. For many applications, the expense in the lattice ball scaling regime can be substantially smaller than that of the hypercube scaling regime, making accurate approximations more accessible. Numerical illustrations include classifying images of handwritten digits from the MNIST dataset and learning the solution operators of partial differential equations, such as Burgers' equation and the radiative transfer equation. The potential for connections with artificial neural networks and other machine learning methods is also discussed.

Explicit Regularization for Flow Matching

Lars Ruthotto, Eran Treister

Learning the underlying probability distribution from samples is a core problem in statistics and applied mathematics. Generative models based on diffusion and flow matching learn a velocity field whose flow transports a known distribution to the data distribution. They produce novel, realistic samples, although the empirical learning problem is ill posed near the data distribution. Its minimizer transports the samples onto the training data, and small perturbations of the data change the velocity field strongly near the end of the flow. The observed generalization is often attributed to implicit regularization by the network parameterization, early stopping of the training, and the time integration during sampling. Explicit regularization has advanced the theory and computation of many inverse problems and has recently been proposed to prevent memorization in generative models. With a regularizer on the magnitude of the velocity, however, the field approaches the memorizing velocity unless the regularization parameter grows to infinity fast enough, and it then stays bounded by a constant that is small only for large parameters. We show that penalizing the derivatives of order $k$ of the velocity renders the $D$-dimensional empirical problem well posed uniformly in time if $2k > D$ and the training points are in general position. Our experiments in one and two dimensions discretize the velocity with splines and use direct solvers for the regularized least-squares problems to exclude optimization error. Refining the discretization in space and time changes the error of the Hessian-regularized flows only marginally, whereas without the regularizer the error tends to that of memorization. These flows generalize, with errors comparable to or lower than those of well-tuned kernel density estimates.

PISG: Constraint-Aligned Signal Amplification for Diffusion-Based Combinatorial Optimization

Yuming Zhang, Xianchen Zhou, Hongxia Wang

Diffusion-based solvers have become a prominent approach to neural combinatorial optimization. At inference time, they assign each decision variable a prediction score. A greedy decoder ranks candidates by these scores and checks hard constraints to ensure feasibility. Top-ranked candidates should be both promising and jointly feasible, making prediction-constraint alignment critical. When this alignment is weak, greedy decoding skips constraint-violating candidates and falls back to lower-ranked alternatives, degrading solution quality. Existing test-time methods improve search, sampling, or decoding, but do not directly strengthen this alignment before decoding. We observe that trained denoisers already carry constraint-aligned structural signals. Building on this observation, we propose Perturbed Instance-Structure Guidance (PISG), a training-free method that amplifies these signals at test time. At each denoising step, PISG constructs a structure-degraded prediction and extrapolates the unperturbed prediction away from it. The resulting guided prediction improves candidate rankings before constrained decoding. Signal analysis shows that this guidance concentrates on constraint-critical variables rather than uniformly rescaling scores. Under greedy decoding and without downstream refinement, PISG improves all 16 TSP solver-scale pairs across DIFUSCO, T2T, FastT2T, and StruDiCO, and all four MIS-ER solvers, with MIS-ER solved-cost gains up to +4.8%. The gains persist on most large-scale settings after 2-opt, and a preliminary study extends PISG to a Transformer-based solver. These results identify prediction-constraint alignment as an effective test-time enhancement direction for diffusion-based combinatorial optimization. Code is available at https://github.com/Ming-321/PISG.

Variationally correct physics-informed operator learning for nonlinear partial differential equations

Yuan Qiu, Wolfgang Dahmen, Peng Chen

Physics-informed operator learning trains neural operators on the residual of a parametric partial differential equation (PDE) instead of labeled solution data, but its reliability depends on a variationally correct residual: one that is equivalent to the error in a norm dictated by the equation, the PDE-compliant norm. For nonlinear equations written as first-order systems, we prove that the first-order system least-squares residual is equivalent to the error in this norm near a solution branch if the linearized residual is stable and the residual derivative is Lipschitz continuous, possibly in a norm stronger than the error norm. We verify these conditions for monotone reaction, solution- and gradient-dependent diffusion, and compressible Neo-Hookean hyperelasticity. The stability mechanisms vary across these classes, as do the regions where the equivalence holds, from arbitrary bounded sets to a neighborhood of the branch. In a conforming finite element space, we show that the loss excess of a state near the finite element solution is equivalent to its squared distance from that solution. We use this loss to train a reduced basis neural operator with values in this space, with solution snapshots needed only for the basis and the loss and its symbolically derived gradient assembled by the finite element library. At inference, this loss estimates the error. Numerically, the square root of the loss excess tracks the error within factors comparable to those of the linear case, and the operator achieves higher accuracy in the PDE-compliant norm than grid-based baselines evaluated in the same finite element space.

From Objective Compromise to Structure-Enabled Intelligence: Reshaping the Scientific Paradigm of AI

Shengchang Chen

Mainstream artificial intelligence systems, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), are fundamentally constructed upon the Optimization-Driven (OD) paradigm. This paradigm can be characterized as an ill-posed optimization problem within a high-dimensional parameter space. When confronted with real-world scenarios characterized by noise and conflicting constraints, the logic of global error minimization inherently induces a strong smoothing effect. Consequently, this yields statistical compromise solutions that flatten local features, making it arduous to overcome the inherent bottleneck of Out-of-Distribution (OOD) generalization. Existing AI models generate outputs purely based on statistical probabilities. They lack the capacity to autonomously discern truth from falsehood or establish causal logic, thereby lacking genuine cognitive intelligence. To address the ontological limitations of the OD paradigm, this paper proposes a novel paradigm termed \textit{Structure-Enabled Intelligence}. This approach abandons the conventional path of relying solely on computing power scaling and data fitting to achieve emergent intelligence. Instead, it embeds mathematical structures and physical mechanisms as underlying priors into the algorithmic architecture, constraining the model's solving process with structured scientific rules. Through this paradigm shift from ``data-driven objective fitting'' to ``mechanism-structure dominance,'' the proposed approach fundamentally mitigates the issue of statistical compromise, enhances the model's extrapolation and generalization capabilities, and simultaneously reshapes human-AI synergy by upholding human subjectivity in scientific cognition.

Structure-Preserving Neural Time-Stepping for Gradient-Flow PDEs via Onsager's Variational Principle

Zhonghua Qiao, Xianmin Xu, Rukang You, Tao Zhou

We propose a unified Onsager-based neural time-stepping (ONTS) framework for solving gradient-flow partial differential equations with conserved and nonconserved dynamics. Unlike conventional neural time-stepping methods that directly parameterize the evolving state, the proposed framework employs spatial neural networks to approximate the Onsager process variables, namely, the rates in nonconserved systems and the fluxes in conserved systems. Within this Eulerian framework, we develop three complementary schemes: an explicit least-squares scheme (E-ONTS), an implicit variational scheme (I-ONTS), and a predictor--corrector scheme (PC-ONTS) that combines explicit prediction with safeguarded implicit correction. For conserved systems, the flux-based formulation guarantees discrete mass conservation under periodic or no-flux boundary conditions. Furthermore, we rigorously establish unconditional discrete energy stability for the implicit schemes, provided that a suitable descent criterion is satisfied, without requiring global minimization of the neural variational objective. Numerical experiments for the heat, Allen--Cahn, Cahn--Hilliard, Fokker--Planck, and coupled Poisson--Nernst--Planck equations demonstrate the accuracy, robustness, and structure-preserving properties of the proposed framework.

Physics-informed neural network for model-based global computation of the asymptotic phase of limit-cycle oscillators

Petar Mircheski, Hiroya Nakao

The asymptotic phase is the central object of phase reduction theory for limit-cycle oscillators. It is a scalar coordinate defined throughout the basin of attraction of a limit cycle that advances at a constant natural frequency along every trajectory. Computing the phase from a given mathematical model requires solving a first-order partial differential equation (PDE) over the entire basin, which is challenging due to high computation costs and complex boundary conditions. In this study, we propose a physics-informed neural network to efficiently calculate the asymptotic phase of limit-cycle oscillators. By incorporating three types of loss functions, we train a physics-informed neural network (PINN) to satisfy the governing PDE for the asymptotic phase and the adjoint equation for the phase gradient. The trained network delivers the phase function accurately and, via automatic differentiation, allows the calculation of the phase gradient governing the oscillator's response to perturbations, which enables rapid entrainment closed-loop control using strong inputs. We validate the approach using three types of limit-cycle oscillators and demonstrate that the PINN can facilitate fast and global phase-locking control.

Variance Stable Gaussian Smoothing Estimator for Arbitrary High-order Derivatives

Shuixin Fang, Xuerui Wang, Hang Zeng, Tao Zhou

Computing high-order partial derivatives in high dimensions by automatic differentiation can be costly in both computation and memory. Stein's identity offers an alternative through Gaussian smoothing, but the variance of the resulting estimators can diverge as the step size tends to zero, even with antithetic sampling. We propose a variance stabilization technique that extends antithetic sampling from a reflection pair to $n$ equally spaced points on a circle in the complex plane. Under local analyticity, we prove that, when $n$ is at least the derivative order, the bias is of order $O(σ^n)$ and the variance remains bounded as the smoothing step size $σ$ tends to zero. Numerical experiments support these predictions and demonstrate the effectiveness of the estimator in physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) for polyharmonic and Cahn--Hilliard equations. The technique mitigates the bias-variance trade-off in smoothing-based derivative estimation and is useful for PINNs solving high-order partial differential equations.

The weak residual requires no derivatives of the networks, the boundary and convection terms carry no penalty or stabilization weights, and the scalar trial functions may jump across the midlines of the primal cells.
