PatternMiner: Automated Mathematical Discovery Through LLM-Guided Training and Interpretation of Small Models

Michael Ruofan Zeng, Junaid Hasan, Xuanyu Yang, Emily Xia Meng, Henry Kvinge

There are several approaches to AI-driven mathematical discovery. One of these involves the use of large language models (LLMs) to resolve well-specified conjectures via text-based reasoning, while another uses narrow models and interpretability for more open-ended, human-in-the-loop exploration using object-based mathematical data. The latter is limited in its ability to move beyond simple empirical pattern detection while the former is not well-designed for direct, open-ended exploration of large mathematical datasets. In this paper we introduce PatternMiner, a system that combines these two paradigms. In PatternMiner, an LLM agent generates mathematical datasets based on an initial broad question, trains narrow machine learning models on them, uses interpretability techniques to extract patterns from these trained models, and uses these patterns to formulate conjectures. Once an initial conjecture has been formulated, PatternMiner instructs the agent to search for counterexamples using calls to mathematical software. If a set of counterexamples can be found, these are used as feedback for conjecture refinement or another round of training and interpretability. The conjecture progressively evolves through this iterative sequence with the agent allowed to try to prove it at any point. We describe the results of applying PatternMiner to five well-known positivity problems in algebraic combinatorics. PatternMiner proves several new theorems related to Schubert positivity and projection, a character-row evaluation formula, a restricted version of the Kazhdan--Lusztig combinatorial invariance conjecture, and Schur positivity for a family of immanant coefficients. We end by analyzing the most common routes to conjecture generation that are used by PatternMiner in our case studies as well as documenting the most common failure modes.

Global Convergence of Second-Order Methods for Physics-Informed Neural Networks in the Overparameterized Limit

Konstantin Riedl, Justin Sirignano, Konstantinos Spiliopoulos

Solving high-dimensional partial differential equations (PDEs) constitutes a core task in many engineering and scientific disciplines. Physics-informed neural networks and the deep Galerkin method effectively leverage machine learning models to approximate solutions of known PDEs by minimizing residuals corresponding to the PDE operator alongside external constraints. Although second-order optimizers are becoming increasingly popular for training neural networks (NNs) to solve PDEs and empirically outperform their first-order counterparts in both speed and accuracy, the convergence theory for training such models is essentially confined to first-order methods. In this paper, we close this gap and develop a convergence analysis for the regularized Newton and Gauss-Newton methods. As the number of hidden units tends to infinity, the training dynamics converge in probability, with explicit rates, to the solution of a deterministic limit equation involving a Newton neural tangent kernel operator associated with the PDE. Although the limiting kernel operator admits no spectral gap, we prove that, in the overparameterized limit, the PDE residual vanishes as training proceeds, and, under mild assumptions, the NN converges to the PDE solution. We further show that, provided a sufficiently small regularization parameter, the projection of the residual onto any fixed number of leading eigenfunctions decays exponentially fast, demonstrating that this hyperparameter acts as a spectral filter: it lifts the convergence rates of high-frequency components toward those of low-frequency ones, thereby mitigating the spectral bias inherent in gradient descent. Mathematical challenges arise from the implicit parameter update involving a linear system whose dimension increases with the NN width and, for Newton's method, a potentially indefinite Hessian, as well as from the PDE operator entering the kernel.

Variational Principles and Machine Learning: a Numerical Analysis and PDE perspective

Charalambos G. Makridakis

Variational principles have played a fundamental role in the development of modern mathematics, physics, and scientific computing. From the principles of least action in mechanics to the calculus of variations and the variational formulation of partial differential equations, they have provided a powerful framework for understanding complex problems and designing reliable numerical methods. In recent years, Machine Learning has introduced a new computational paradigm, in which highly expressive nonlinear function spaces and large-scale minimisation problems play a central role. In these notes, we explore the connection between these two frameworks. We develop a variational and numerical-analysis perspective on Machine Learning algorithms, focusing in particular on their formulation as nonlinear minimisation problems. We investigate how classical concepts such as stability, consistency, approximability, and compactness acquire new interpretations in this setting, and how the variational structure of the underlying continuous problem can govern the behaviour of neural-network approximations. This perspective provides a mathematical framework for understanding existing ML algorithms while also suggesting new approaches to their design. In particular, alternative and strongly coercive variational principles can lead to more stable, reliable, and potentially more efficient computational methods.

Exact Integration of ReLU-type Neural Networks for Solving PDEs

Shima Baharlouei, Ignacio Brevis, Reuben Menezes, David Pardo, Kristoffer G. van der Zee

Developing robust training methodologies for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) with neural networks is crucial for reliable scientific computing. Piecewise-linear activations such as the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) are a convenient choice in this setting as their structure resembles classical piecewise-linear approximations. However, when the loss functional incorporates the spatial gradient of the network, as in the Deep Ritz method, numerical integration becomes problematic. Specifically, relying on standard integration techniques (e.g., Monte Carlo sampling) during training yields incorrect gradients, even as the number of integration points approaches infinity, leading to optimization failure. To overcome this limitation, we present an integration framework for single-hidden-layer ReLU networks that computes correct gradients when using Deep Ritz for PDEs or PINNs for first-order problems. Our approach employs the Sutherland-Hodgman algorithm to partition the physical domain into subdomains according to breaking hyperplanes underlying the ReLU network. By finding these hyperplanes exactly, our method can correctly integrate the gradients, ensuring the optimizer receives correct information. In this work, we further assemble the energy in matrix--vector form that allows all neuron interactions to be computed efficiently with vectorized operations, introduce an alternative approach termed Conforming Monte Carlo that is a stochastic version of the proposed method, and propose two stochastic training strategies to reduce complexity. Numerical results on two-dimensional domains demonstrate that our framework recovers optimal PDE solutions that standard stochastic sampling and other existing quadrature methods fail to capture.

Two 3-Dimensional Coefficient Inverse Problems for Wave-like Equations With a Single Location of the Point Source

Michael V. Klibanov, Jingzhi Li, Tian Niu

In this paper two long standing open questions are addressed for the first time. Those questions are about Lipschitz stability estimates and uniqueness theorems for two Coefficient Inverse Problems (CIPs) for 3-Dimensional wave-like Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) with formally determined input data. We assume that a certain associated PDE is written in finite differences with respect to time t. The unknown coefficients depend on all three spatial variables. The key novelty here is that the input data for these CIPs are generated by only a single location of the point source at the moment of time {t = 0}. In the first CIP (CIP1) the unknown coefficient is the potential of a wave-like PDE. The second CIP (CIP2) is concerned with a far more challenging case when the unknown coefficient is involved in the principal part of the hyperbolic operator. In addition to Lipschitz stability and uniqueness results, numerical methods are developed for both CIPs. Their convergence analysis establishes the global convergence property of both numerical techniques. The latter is unlike the local convergence of conventional numerical procedures for CIPs. The method for CIP1 is tested computationally. Its extensive numerical testing for complicated media structures consistently demonstrates a very accurate performance of this method for noisy input data. The authors declare that artificial intelligence was not used for any part of this work.

EPRO: Robust Policy Optimization Through an Imperfect Execution-Policy Predictor

Yibo Wang, Changyi Yang, Yuanxin Liu, Zhaoran Wang

RL for LLMs often separates rollout generation from policy training. Although the two numerical engines share model parameters, they can induce different policies. This creates a challenge beyond correcting a known training-inference mismatch: during learner updates, the execution policy corresponding to the current parameters cannot be directly observed. We view this as optimizing an unobserved execution policy through an imperfect predictor of its evolution. This perspective raises two fundamental questions: how can we estimate the execution policy's response to learner updates, and how much should we trust that estimate when optimizing the actual execution policy? We introduce Execution-Policy Robust Optimization (EPRO) to address these challenges. Starting from the observed rollout-time execution policy, we use training-policy changes to construct a differentiable predictor of the current execution policy, whose score yields Transported Score Centering (TSC). Our robust-improvement analysis reveals how prediction error limits the magnitude of reliably justified updates. For practical implementation, we introduce Policy Shift as an observable proxy for prediction risk and use Shift-Aware Soft Clipping (SSC) to attenuate risky updates. Across four Qwen3 model settings, EPRO achieves the highest average held-out accuracy among the compared methods on seven mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

U-Statistics-Powered LLM Reasoning

Shijin Gong, Kai Ye, Victor Chernozhukov, Xinyu Zhang, Chengchun Shi

Since its introduction by DeepSeek, group relative policy optimization (GRPO) has been widely adopted for training large reasoning models and agents, spurring a proliferation of its variants. Yet the group size, a crucial hyperparameter in these GRPO-type algorithms, remains largely chosen by trial and error. Exploiting the U-statistic structure of GRPO's policy gradient, we recast the selection of the group size as a classical statistical problem of variance ratio estimation. This problem is particularly challenging when the denominator of the ratio is small, rendering naive plug-in estimators unstable. To address this challenge, we propose a pessimistic ratio estimator that accounts for the denominator's estimation uncertainty. We characterise the statistical properties of this estimator across three sample-size regimes. Empirically, using %a jackknife-based pessimistic rule on synthetic tasks from Reasoning Gym and real-world benchmarks covering both mathematical reasoning and code generation, we show that our adaptive group size selection reduces the mean squared error of the resulting policy gradient and improves policy optimisation over fixed-group-size baselines, with modest additional computational cost.

SGD with Large Learning Rates: General Analysis & Improved Rates

Konstantinos Emmanouilidis, Ziqing Xu, Salma Tarmoun, Lachlan MacDonald, Rene Vidal

Stochastic Gradient Descent lies at the core of modern machine learning. However its classical analysis typically restricts the stepsize to be below a curvature-dependent stability threshold. Recent developments in deterministic optimization have challenged this paradigm, showing that suitably chosen large stepsizes can not only preserve convergence, but also accelerate Gradient Descent (GD). Whether this phenomenon persists in the presence of stochastic gradient noise has remained an open question (Wu et al., 2024). In this work, we answer this question affirmatively for the cross-entropy loss with linear predictors and two-layer neural networks, establishing an accelerated $\mathcal{O}(T^{-2})$ convergence rate. At the core of our analysis is a fine-grained characterization of the stochastic large-stepsize dynamics. This sharper control of the trajectory further yields tight convergence guarantees within the stable regime, improving existing bounds even in the deterministic setting. Taken together, our results show that the acceleration mechanism induced by large stepsizes survives stochasticity and extends beyond linear models. Numerical experiments demonstrate the benefits of large learning rates in practice.

DLaR: Dynamic Stochasticity Control for Latent Reasoning in Large Language Models

Xiaocheng Luo, Peng Xu, Zaifu Zhan, Xiangyu Duan

Latent reasoning enables LLMs to operate in continuous embedding space, yet existing methods suffer from deterministic inference that precludes exploration of diverse reasoning paths. While recent approaches inject stochasticity to restore exploration, we identify two fundamental limitations: \textit{uniform stochasticity} across all steps is suboptimal, and unconstrained perturbations disrupt reasoning coherence. We propose Dynamic Stochasticity Control for Latent Reasoning (DLaR), a framework with two complementary mechanisms that operate at different temporal granularities within the GRPO reinforcement learning framework. At the token level, Adaptive Stochasticity Modulation (ASM) dynamically adjusts perturbation intensity at each latent step based on a confidence-entropy fusion signal, increasing exploration when the model is uncertain and preserving stability when confident. Through this dual-level design, DLaR establishes a hierarchical exploration-exploitation strategy that operates at both token and training levels. Large language models (LLMs) have been explored for generating optimization models from natural language, but their reliability in end-to-end settings remains limited, often producing non-executable, infeasible, or feasible but incorrect solutions. In this work, we study failure modes in LLM-based optimization modeling by decomposing the process into three components: mathematical reasoning, solver usage, and domain knowledge, and constructing datasets that target each aspect. These findings highlight limitations of current dataset designs and suggest the importance of data that better capture constraint completeness and formulation accuracy to enhance reliability.

Efficient Data Fusion in Distributionally Robust Optimization

Buse Şen, Aras Selvi, Jean Pauphilet, Daniel Kuhn

Decisions under uncertainty increasingly rest on fragmented evidence, such as samples from related but different populations, privacy-protected summary statistics, or complementary statistical descriptions of the same data. Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) can fuse such evidence by constructing one ambiguity set per source and hedging against all distributions in the intersection of these sets. This fusion approach is less conservative than aggregating the sources into a single ambiguity set, but it is computationally demanding. Exact reformulations grow exponentially with the number of sources, and even deciding whether the intersection is nonempty is NP-hard. We show that fusion DRO admits an exact reformulation in which the ambiguity sets are coupled only through functional Lagrange multipliers. Restricting these multipliers to affine decision rules yields upper bounds that decompose across the ambiguity sets, are exact for quadratic losses over Wasserstein balls around Gaussian references, and provably dominate aggregation-based relaxations such as Minkowski sums and balls around mixtures. The approximations admit finite conic reformulations in important special cases and subgradient methods for weakly convex losses, which makes fusion DRO applicable to neural network training. In portfolio, newsvendor, and binary classification experiments, the affine approximation stays close to the exact value wherever the latter is computable, scales far better than exact methods, and outperforms aggregation-based benchmarks out-of-sample.

Decision-Focused Learning for Multistage Stochastic Programs, with Application to Power Network Repair Scheduling

David Krame Kadurha, Alessandro Zocca, Sandjai Bhulai

Multistage stochastic programs (MSPs) with discrete decisions are hard to solve due to the curse of dimensionality, non-convex value functions, and scenario trees that grow exponentially with the horizon. We develop a decision-focused learning (DFL) framework for MSPs in which a neural network predicts scores for the part of the decision that drives the state transition. Under structural conditions that make this sub-decision enter the stage objective linearly, the network is trained on labels from offline single-scenario problems, using the Fenchel-Young loss with perturbed optimizers, which need only an optimization oracle. We apply the framework to repair scheduling in power networks under stochastic, time-varying component failures, with and without a per-stage repair budget. On a synthetic 5-bus network and on the IEEE 14-bus network at two repair-cost scales, the learned and baseline policies are compared on common evaluation paths. Without the budget, DFL is the cheapest policy on the 5-bus network; on the 14-bus network it does not outperform a near-sighted model-predictive control (MPC) baseline, but it beats repairing every failed component at the higher scale. Under a binding budget, DFL is the cheapest policy evaluated on both networks and at both scales, 14% to 23% below the next-best policy. On the 5-bus network, the mean cost of DFL is 1.7% (without the budget) and 1.8% (with it) above the exact optimum computed by dynamic programming. Within days of computation, sample average approximation yields costlier policies than DFL and the repair-everything heuristic, and stochastic dual dynamic integer programming remains far from convergence. DFL computes its decisions about as fast as the trivial heuristics and orders of magnitude faster than MPC. Since these conditions are not specific to power networks, the framework extends to other MSPs that share this structure.

FokasNets: Completing missing boundary data in Physics-Informed Neural Networks and Operators via the Global Relation

Kyriakos C. Georgiou, Khemraj Shukla, Athanassios S. Fokas, George Em Karniadakis

We introduce FokasNets, a framework that combines Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and operators (PINOs) with the Global Relation (GR) formulation of the Fokas method (also referred to as the Unified Transform), for mixed boundary problems. The general framework considers lifted representations of the neural network models, where the Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed by construction and the neural network learns the interior correction. The Global Relation is used as an additional solve during training that provides the missing boundary conditions to the lifted neural network, making training more efficient and accurate in the interior and on the boundary. We develop the construction of FokasNets for elliptic and evolution PDEs (Fokas-PINNs), and the corresponding PDE solution operator problems (Fokas Operator Networks - FONets), using the Global Relation formulations for these PDE classes. Furthermore, we develop the necessary theoretical framework for FokasNets, including error and stability analysis and show that FokasNets are universal approximators. We provide comprehensive numerical experiments, using examples with manufactured solutions and literature benchmarks. Compared to standard PINNs and Physics-Informed DeepONets and their lifted versions, FokasNets achieve significant improvements in solution accuracy in the interior and boundaries, both for single PDEs and particularly for solution operators, where the improvement reaches multiple orders of magnitude and fixed threshold errors are reached faster during training.

Jan Rohleff, Johannes Schropp

Nonlinear mixed-effects (NLME) models are the central statistical models of pharmacometrics, where they support dose selection, individualized dosing and regulatory decisions in drug development. Statistically, NLME modeling is a marginalized likelihood problem. Variational autoencoders (VAEs) solve such problems with a neural network encoder that amortizes the approximation of the intractable posterior distribution, and allow the integration of AI components into NLME modeling. This paper provides the convergence analysis of the VAE-NLME algorithm. First, its version for the evidence lower bound (ELBO) is proven to converge almost surely to the set of stationary points of the ELBO, without assuming that the encoder is trained exactly. Second, the final VAE-NLME algorithm trains the encoder on the ELBO by gradient-based optimization and uses it as proposal for importance-weighted sufficient statistics. It converges almost surely to the set of stationary points of the marginal log-likelihood, without any assumption on the training of the encoder. Thus, it combines the fast gradient-based training of VAEs with the limit of maximum likelihood estimation.

Mojtaba Soltanalian

Additional observations can make exact optimization tractable without changing the objective or feasible set. We establish this phenomenon for noisy parity fitting, a worst-case NP-hard problem, with uniformly sampled rank-one sign tensor entries, independent fair factor signs and independent errors. Routing and Cache Retention for Multi-Turn LLM Inference: Theory and Implementation

Sentao Miao, Tianjiao Nie, Yuan Zhou, Song Yuan, Yuanwei Lu, Yibo Zhu

Multi-turn AI agents execute iterative loops of large language model (LLM) inference and external tool calls, transforming cluster serving into a dynamic resource-allocation problem where routing and key-value (KV) cache retention jointly govern prefix reuse across inter-turn delays. We formulate a fluid approximation of this system and show that optimal admission, routing, and eviction are governed by endpoint-specific shadow prices for computation and memory together with session continuation values. Building on this structure, we propose an asymptotically optimal buffer-reserve policy that absorbs stochastic fluctuations using a vanishing share of capacity. To handle asynchronous arrivals, delayed load reports, and engine-local memory management in production clusters, we translate these principles into a practical controller named PARK, which pairs online projected subgradient price updates and one-turn migration lookahead with a local KV retention index ranked by expected reuse value per byte-epoch. In real GPU cluster experiments serving a 197-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model on multi-turn software-engineering agent benchmarks (SWE and Terminal-Bench), PARK outperforms all five load-balancing and prefix-aware baselines across contrasting traffic regimes. On 16 H200 GPUs, PARK raises output throughput by $24.2\%$ over the strongest baseline ($336.9\%$ over round robin) on turn-intensive SWE trajectories and by $40.0\%$ over the strongest baseline ($63.3\%$ over round robin) on generation-intensive Terminal-Bench trajectories. Controlled single-replica ablations show that our retention index alone cuts prior-prefix token loss from $13.26\%$ to $2.17\%$ and increases throughput by $36.7\%$ over least-recently-used (LRU) eviction, while 128-GPU scale-up experiments sustain 50,789 to 67,363 output tokens/s across 16~replicas with prefix cache hit rates of $91.3\%$ to $97.4\%$.

Organizations use large language models (LLMs) to classify jobs that differ in uncertainty and the cost of errors. Querying additional models may improve accuracy, but it also incurs fees and increases congestion at shared servers. We study congestion-aware adaptive routing (CAAR) policies that adapt model selection and stopping decisions to observed outputs while accounting for the steady-state congestion they generate. The objective is to minimize the long-run average cost of misclassification penalties, query fees, and holding costs. We can represent CAAR policies as a mixture of decision trees, and derive exact holding costs for first-come-first-served servers with exponential service times and processor-sharing servers with general service times. We formulate a convex optimization problem with unique optimal model loads. Congestion prices capture the marginal system-wide holding costs of additional queries and guide routing and stopping decisions through a Bayesian dynamic program. We characterize optimal stopping decisions and identify models whose information does not justify their cost. A column-generation algorithm computes optimal CAAR policies and provides an explicit optimality-gap bound. We also bound the additional cost of limiting cascade depth relative to full-depth CAAR. Finally, under a common query-depth limit, cascades with a predetermined model order can be arbitrarily more costly than optimal CAAR, even when the order is randomized and stopping decisions adapt to observed outputs. Numerical experiments distinguish the benefits of adaptive model selection and congestion-aware design.

Convergence and error analysis of problem-independent machine learning (PIML) with linear completeness

Ke Tan, Chong-Jun Li, Xu Guo

Problem-independent machine learning (PIML) is an effective computational approach that combines the finite element method with machine learning. PIML uses neural networks to learn the mapping from material distributions or element geometries to the numerical shape functions of substructures. This approach avoids repeated computation while preserving the conformity of the underlying finite element discretization, thereby significantly improving the efficiency of substructure-based finite element analysis. However, existing studies on PIML have primarily focused on numerical accuracy and computational efficiency, while rigorous theoretical analyses of its approximation errors are still limited. In this paper, we establish an error analysis framework for PIML from a classical finite element perspective, in which the numerical error is bounded in terms of the finite element discretization error and the network generalization error. Our analysis shows that, when only rigid-body displacement invariance is enforced, the resulting error estimate contains a learning-induced term that does not necessarily decrease under mesh refinement. To ensure convergence, we propose an improved PIML scheme with linear completeness. By using linear displacement field constraints to reconstruct the corresponding columns of the transition matrix, we ensure that the resulting numerical shape-function space contains the space of linear polynomials. We derive error estimates and establish convergence results of the improved scheme in the H1 and L2 norms. Numerical experiments verify the theoretical results and demonstrate that the improved PIML achieves optimal convergence rates, good generalization performance, and numerical stability.
