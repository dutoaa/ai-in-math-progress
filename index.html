TriGeoBench: A Multimodal Mathematical Geometry Benchmark with Figures and TikZ

Jing Wang, Yitong Yang, Hao Dou, Yaomin Wu, Shuting He, Tingfei Shen

Multimodal Large Language Models show strong vision-language capabilities, yet their geometry-centric reasoning remains insufficiently understood. Existing mathematical benchmarks typically rely on rasterized figures and cover limited geometric knowledge areas and response formats, making it difficult to assess whether models reason over geometric structures or exploit superficial visual patterns. We introduce TriGeoBench, a geometry-centric multimodal benchmark constructed from Chinese middle- and high-school mathematics examinations. TriGeoBench contains 7,000 question-answer pairs and 12,235 geometric figures with TikZ code, spanning plane geometry, solid geometry, analytic geometry, function, and vector geometry across diverse response formats. Each instance forms a {question-answer pair, image, TikZ} triplet: question-answer pairs are stored in LaTeX, images as RGB figures, and TikZ preserves geometric topology, symbolic annotations, and relational structure. We evaluate representative open- and closed-source MLLMs through image-to-TikZ and multimodal reasoning probes. Results reveal persistent fine-grained structure and image-text alignment challenges. Oracle experiments with human-curated reference TikZ suggest that accurate executable representations provide complementary geometric cues; they do not evaluate an end-to-end conversion pipeline. TriGeoBench offers a testbed for structure-aware mathematical reasoning.

Reinforcement Learning for Global-Local Control of Dynamic Networks with Applications to Systemic Risk

Nils Detering, Florian Grell, Thilo Meyer-Brandis

We propose a reinforcement learning framework for solving control problems on dynamic networks, where control decisions consist of a combination of graph-level (global) and node-level (local) actions. To exploit the underlying network structure in a policy-gradient algorithm, we introduce a message-passing neural network architecture with separate global and local policy heads. The architecture ensures that the resulting policies satisfy natural permutation symmetries and transparency constraints. Our main theoretical contribution is an analysis of the robustness and regularity properties of the proposed architecture. We derive explicit Lipschitz bounds with respect to both parameter and input perturbations and use these estimates to establish convergence guarantees for a projected policy-gradient algorithm. The resulting constants reveal how architectural choices and graph characteristics influence sensitivity to input perturbations and the convergence behavior of gradient-based optimization methods. As an application, we study the management of systemic risk in financial networks, where a regulator allocates capital interventions across interconnected institutions. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed reinforcement learning architecture outperforms standard architectures based on feedforward neural networks while the learned policies fulfill desirable symmetry and transparency constraints. The algorithm scales to large networks and effectively combines global budget decisions with local allocation policies by exploiting the underlying graph structure.

ORSMT: Unleashing the Potential of Satisfiability Modulo Theories for Semantic Verification and Repair in LLM-Based Optimization

Rui Zhang, Chenyu Zhou, Jianghao Lin, Yaoxin Wu, Dongdong Ge, Wen Song

Large language models increasingly automate optimization modeling from natural language problems, producing written mathematics, solver code and a returned report. Verifying one optimization problem across these artifacts requires semantic comparison: unequal models can attain the same objective, equivalent models can use different auxiliary variables, and mutually consistent artifacts can omit the same source requirement. ORSMT provides a typed semantic interface and exact SMT relation queries. It aligns observable decisions, projects auxiliary variables, proves or refutes feasible region and objective relations, localizes refutations to implicated rows or domains and links source texts when available. It then reconstructs one artifact from another, and each adopted candidate passes the required relation checks. Across 3,034 problems and six generator sizes, ORSMT improves the model-code match rate in all 72 benchmark and generator combinations before selection. Protected selection raises the aggregate rate from 43.2% to 88.2% and the share that are both correct and matched from 25.2% to 40.2%. On the 1,905 refuted outputs, certified voting under the shared fallback makes 50.9% of outputs correct and matched, compared with 44.8% for ReLoop and 49.4% for Opt-Verifier. A separate one-round source revision followed by exact reconstruction restores a model-code match for 84.4% of the refuted pairs.

EPIC: Rethinking Trust Regions for Autoregressive LLM Policies via Exact-KL Prefix Importance-Ratio Clipping

Chengxi Li, Hanlin Wu, Zhicheng Cai, Huan-ang Gao, Wei-Ying Ma, Jingjing Liu

Reinforcement learning (RL) based on PPO-style clipping has become an effective approach to improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). However, standard token-level clipping measures policy change only through the conditional probability of the current token, while the prefix conditioning context is itself generated by the policy. This mismatch becomes especially consequential in long-response and off-policy training. We reformulate autoregressive generation as a finite-horizon Markov decision process and derive its performance-difference identity under behavior-policy sampling. The resulting change of measure identifies the cumulative likelihood ratio of the generated prefix as the exact importance weight for each stepwise policy-improvement term. Starting from the monotonic-improvement principle of trust-region policy optimization, we then construct a prefix-level trust region using the exact reverse-KL coordinate $r-1-\log r$, whose behavior-policy expectation equals the corresponding prefix-distribution KL divergence. This yields EPIC, a PPO-style sequence-clipping method that directly regulates prefix-level policy drift without relying on a local quadratic approximation or expert-routing replay. Experiments on off-policy reinforcement learning with a sparse mixture-of-experts language model show that EPIC consistently outperforms standard and sequence-level clipping baselines across mathematical-reasoning benchmarks, while remaining competitive with routing-replay-based alternatives. Code is available at https://github.com/LiChengxi666/EPIC.

Minimax Rates for imperfect Physics-Informed Regression

Nathan Doumèche

Physics-informed machine learning incorporates physical knowledge into data-driven models, often through a partial differential equation (PDE) that the target is assumed to satisfy. However, how much this knowledge improves statistical performance remains unclear, especially when the physical model is imperfect. Since satisfying a PDE implies being differentiable, a natural benchmark for physics-informed regression is the best achievable performance when smoothness is the only prior information about the target function. We show that there is a characteristic threshold for the modeling error above which the physical prior cannot improve the convergence rate. The form of this threshold is common to every linear PDE and certain nonlinear PDEs. Below it, the gain depends on the equation type. For classes of elliptic and hyperbolic PDEs with constant coefficients, we establish convergence rates that improve continuously as the modeling error decreases, and prove their minimax optimality up to logarithmic factors. With exact physics, the rates correspond to learning in dimension d-1 rather than d, reflecting the freedom left by unknown boundary or initial data. We also give conditions for computing the estimator with near-linear complexity and show how to tune its hyperparameters without knowing the modeling error.

Convergence of stochastic gradient descent for learning linear neural networks

Gabin Maxime Nguegnang, Bubacarr Bah, Holger Rauhut, Ulrich Terstiege

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is the cornerstone of modern deep learning, yet its theoretical convergence properties remain poorly understood in non-convex settings. To address this gap, we study SGD dynamics in deep linear neural networks - simplified models that retain key non-convex characteristics while remaining analytically tractable. Building on recent results for gradient descent, we extend convergence guarantees to SGD under regularized squared loss. We establish almost sure boundedness of SGD iterates. Using the framework of asymptotic pseudotrajectories from stochastic approximation theory, our analysis establishes then, for both deterministic and adaptive step-size choices, convergence of SGD to the set of critical points under mild conditions (without relying on Lipschitz properties or whitened data). To our knowledge, this is the first work providing almost sure convergence guarantees for SGD in deep linear networks, bridging the gap between theoretical optimization and practical stochastic training.

Group Distributionally Robust Optimization over Decentralized Networks

Haomin Bai, Junyan Sun, Quan Cheng, Yuanyu Wan, Lijun Zhang

We study group distributionally robust optimization (GDRO) over decentralized networks, where workers collaborate to learn robust models by minimizing the maximum local risk. Existing methods optimize only dual-regularized surrogates and establish guarantees for a globally averaged model that is not available to workers. In this paper, we directly optimize the original GDRO objective and establish guarantees for local models in semi-decentralized (SemiDec) and fully decentralized (Dec) settings. A key difficulty is that applying the primal-dual decomposition from centralized GDRO yields worker-dependent losses whose evaluation requires unavailable cross-worker information. To address this challenge, we develop a novel error decomposition that recasts decentralized GDRO as (i) a decentralized online convex optimization (D-OCO) problem and a prediction-with-expert-advice problem in SemiDec, and as (ii) two coupled D-OCO problems in Dec, with discrepancy terms capturing inconsistencies among local decisions. By isolating consensus errors in analysis, we develop modular frameworks that support different consensus routines. We then establish the \emph{first} per-worker convergence guarantees for optimizing the original GDRO objective over decentralized networks. Instantiations with accelerated gossip and Choco-gossip achieve an $O(\sqrt{(\log m)/T})$ convergence rate over $T$ update rounds, while experiments demonstrate improved worst-worker performance over baselines.

A Model-Free Continuous-Time RL Algorithm from Discrete-Time Off-Policy Data with End-to-End Guarantees

Chaorui Yao, Yuming Paul Zhang, Lin Yang, Yuhua Zhu

In this paper, we study continuous-time reinforcement learning, where the state follows an unknown stochastic differential equation but only a fixed off-policy batch of discrete-time transitions is available. Standard discrete-time Q-learning becomes problematic as the sampling interval shrinks, because differences between action values vanish. Continuous-time alternatives, such as Deep Advantage Updating and martingale q-learning, preserve the action signal but jointly learn a value function and an action-dependent critic. Building on the PhiBE-Q formulation (arXiv:2606.21925), we develop a model-free PhiBE policy iteration (PhiBE-PI) framework that uses a single state-action critic while preserving the continuous-time action-ranking scale. At each iteration, the framework learns the critic directly from sampled increments and uses it to improve the policy. The same batch is reused throughout, without explicitly estimating the drift or diffusion. We further develop an implementation under linear function approximation and establish a local end-to-end suboptimality guarantee for the learned policy. Our analysis quantifies how errors from time discretization, function approximation, and finite-sample policy evaluation propagate through policy iteration. It accounts for the dependence of successive policies on the reused data, so the guarantee does not require a fresh batch at each iteration. Moreover, we identify a bias-variance trade-off: smaller sampling intervals improve the approximation to the continuous-time problem but can increase statistical error at a fixed sample size. Thus, finer time discretization does not necessarily lead to better policy performance.

Many scientific phenomena are best understood as outcomes of underlying optimization problems, yet automated scientific discovery has largely focused on recovering equations that describe observed input-output patterns. We introduce OptScientist, a novel automated framework for discovering compact symbolic optimization laws directly from data. OptScientist comprises (i) a model that infers symbolic optimization problems by first learning to represent data as an optimization problem and then extracting its symbolic form, and (ii) an LLM agentic system embedding the model in a discovery process that integrates theoretical priors from scientific literature, discovers candidate laws, abstracts their underlying mechanisms, and suggests new data to collect. On human risky choice and central-bank monetary policy, OptScientist discovers compact symbolic optimization laws that achieve held-out predictive performance competitive with leading baselines, while using few parameters and substantially fewer tokens than LLM baselines. Overall, OptScientist extends automated scientific discovery to uncovering optimization laws that generate observed phenomena, enabling automated theory building across the physical, biological, behavioral, and social sciences.

Soliton Profiles in 1D: Enhanced Neural-Network Solvers and Constrained Deep Ritz Methods

Chandler Haight, Alex D Rodriguez, Svetlana Roudenko

We develop and compare enhanced neural-network solvers for one-dimensional ground-state profiles, which are solutions of $$ Q^{\prime\prime}=bQ-γQ^{p},\quad Q>0,\quad p>1, \quad b, γ>0, $$ the stationary profile equation for the nonlinear Schrödinger, generalized Korteweg-de Vries, and nonlinear Klein-Gordon equations. Its explicit sech-type solutions serve as exact reference for error computation and benchmark hyperparameter selection. We improve on the standard Adam-trained physics-informed neural network (PINN) baseline by introducing higher-order optimization via Gauss-Newton/Levenberg-Marquardt and L-BFGS refinements, as well as Fourier-feature PINNs, and constrained Deep Ritz (DR) formulations. In DR approaches we introduce two new neural-network formulations, which implement two classical variational characterizations: a Nehari-constrained Deep Ritz method, in which the height is learned by projecting onto the Nehari/Pokhozhaev manifold, and a Weinstein Deep Ritz method, in which a normalized profile minimizes the Weinstein quotient. The Deep Ritz formulations perform best overall, in particular, the Weinstein variant gives the best error-runtime balance. The neural-network based solvers remain less accurate and efficient than classical Petviashvili iteration in 1D, however, the current study provides a proof of concept: the mesh-free formulation, non-reliance on exact profiles or the related features make them promising in higher dimensions and for more complicated nonlinearities and dispersive operators.

Curvature-Scaled Sharpness-Aware Minimization: Convergence Beyond Smoothness

Dimitris Oikonomou, Nicolas Loizou

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) has established itself as a widely used method for training deep neural networks. Despite its popularity, existing convergence guarantees for SAM-type methods require smoothness, a restrictive assumption. Generalized smoothness conditions have recently been proposed in the analysis of gradient methods but remain underexplored for SAM. To analyze the effect of SAM's perturbation under generalized smoothness, we use relative gradient distortion to quantify how the sharpness-aware direction departs from the original gradient. Under $(L_0,L_1)$-smoothness, we show that this distortion can become unbounded near stationarity for SAM and at large gradients for its unnormalized variant (USAM), with examples where SAM cycles and USAM diverges. We then establish a necessary condition for uniform distortion control among perturbations along the gradient whose nonnegative multipliers depend only on its norm. Motivated by this condition, we propose Curvature-Scaled SAM (CS-SAM), which scales both the perturbation and the descent step by suitable curvature upper bounds, and prove that it uniformly bounds the distortion. Under both $(L_0,L_1)$- and $(H_0,H_1)$-smoothness, we establish deterministic $\mathcal{O}(1/T)$ rates for the best squared gradient norm on nonconvex objectives, last-iterate $\mathcal{O}(1/T)$ rates for the function gap on convex objectives with a minimizer, and linear convergence under the Polyak-Lojasiewicz condition. With zero perturbation, our guarantees recover the rates for gradient descent with $(L_0,L_1)$- or $(H_0,H_1)$-based step-sizes. Minibatch experiments on computer vision tasks demonstrate competitive performance for both variants, with the $(H_0,H_1)$ variant achieving the highest mean best-test accuracy in all four settings against tuned SAM and USAM with constant and cosine annealing schedules.

Local Work, Global Progress: A Convergence Theory of AI Agent Collaboration

Ivan Ilin, Peter Richtárik

Teams of large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly applied to complex tasks, but their collaboration protocols are mostly designed empirically, without convergence guarantees. We formalize collaborative problem solving as block optimization: each agent improves one part of a shared solution, guided by its local objective plus a correction for how its changes affect the other parts. A coordinator checks every proposal against the global objective and merges proposals that do not interfere with each other. We prove linear convergence up to an error floor that vanishes when the corrections are exact. Interfering proposals can also be merged once their combination has been checked; the rate then depends on how well the proposals actually combine, not on worst-case interactions. With GPT-OSS-120B agents on robot routing, a three-agent team finds collision-free plans in every run, better than those of a single agent that controls all robots. On a larger routing task, a six-agent team completes its search about twice as fast as the single agent (in median). Merging the best checked combination of proposals, rather than only proposals that do not interfere, cuts the team's search time by about 30%. With constants measured on the runs, our convergence bound holds in every round and follows the observed progress. We also test the theory on query planning in SQLite and on a synthetic task.

In addition, we show how the proposed MOR framework can be used to solve seemingly unrelated problems. In particular, we provide an example for fitting high degree polynomials to data and also obtain a FEM-like method for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) with an optimal kernel basis.
