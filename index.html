Atomic MathReasoner: Instance-Level Composition of Atomic Reasoning Skills for Mathematical LLMs

Jiashu Li, Tianyu Gao, Qinglong Liu, Jinping Wang, Ye Wei, Zhiqiang Gao

Most existing approaches enhance mathematical reasoning in large language models (LLMs) as a monolithic capability, despite the fact that different mathematical problems require different reasoning skills. Recent work has explored a more fine-grained perspective by decomposing mathematical reasoning into atomic skills and developing specialized modules for individual capabilities. However, how to effectively utilize these atomic abilities remains underexplored. To address this challenge, we propose Atomic MathReasoner (AMR), an instance-level framework for composing atomic reasoning skills. AMR first trains a library of capability-specific LoRA experts and performs gradient-free optimization over held-out validation subsets to discover a diverse set of low-loss composition prototypes. It evaluates the preference of each prototype using the token-level negative log-likelihood of reference solutions, filters ambiguous assignments through a confidence margin, and distills the resulting pseudo-labels into a lightweight router. During inference, the router selects an appropriate composition prototype for each problem, enabling problem-specific utilization of atomic skills without costly online optimization. Experiments on the AtomicMath benchmark across general and mathematics-specialized LLMs demonstrate consistent improvements. On Qwen2.5-Math-Instruct-7B, AMR increases average field accuracy from 64.4% to 68.1% at Level 1 and from 59.3% to 63.7% at Level 2, while improving average logical reasoning performance from 39.4% to 46.8%. These results demonstrate that effectively composing specialized atomic skills provides a promising approach to strengthening mathematical reasoning in LLMs.

A Systematic Analysis of a Small Optimal Subspace for Physics-Informed Neural Networks: $n$-DAS $\subset \mathbb{R}^p$

Ahmad AliAhmad, Khemraj Shukla

The search for a low-dimensional subspace that governs neural network training has been a persistent goal in optimization. Yet subspaces defined by Hessian eigenvalue magnitude, such as the dominant/bulk decomposition underlying Dom-SGD and Bulk-SGD, have repeatedly failed to capture where optimization takes place, and adaptive Krylov-based alternatives built on energy-fraction thresholds show promise but still struggle at lower eigenmodes. We argue this failure is structural: the directions of highest curvature are not, in general, the directions along which training moves. We introduce the dominant aligned subspace (DAS), constructed from a full eigendecomposition of the Hessian but selected by gradient alignment rather than eigenvalue magnitude, as a tool for understanding optimization dynamics and a foundation for designing methods that exploit them. To expose this structure under the hardest conditions, we use physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) as a testbed: differential operators from the parabolic, elliptic, and hyperbolic classes induce severely ill-conditioned, anisotropic loss landscapes. Across Poisson, heat, wave, and viscous Burgers benchmarks DAS-restricted training with a line search matches full-space training even where the projected Hessian remains ill-conditioned, showing empirically that convergence does not require all Hessian eigendirections but only a low-dimensional, gradient-aligned subset. We extend the analysis to Gauss-Newton within the DAS and discuss what this structure implies for future subspace-aware optimizers.

Structural functional identifiability of unknown differential equation observation maps

Torkel E Loman, Alexander P Browning, Ruth E Baker

In many differential equation models of real-world systems, the mechanistic structure of the dynamics is known, but the map from the dynamics to the observations is not. This is potentially problematic, as it can prevent us from e.g. learning model parameter values from data. Examples where this occurs include measuring a fluorescent marker as a proxy for the dynamics of a cellular circuit, and using wastewater measurements to follow the development of an epidemic. The nascent field of hybrid modelling, in which machine learning components (like neural networks) are embedded within mechanistic models, provides the tools to learn such maps. Here, the system's dynamics keep their mechanistic form, while a neural network represents the unknown observation map and is trained alongside any unknown core system parameters. This raises a question of identifiability: when can the true observation map be learnt, and when does one simply learn some map that transforms the dynamics into the available data? Building on previous work, we show how structural functional identifiability analysis can establish whether a model's unknown observation maps and core system parameters are identifiable, i.e. can be uniquely learnt from data. We both prove general results on when models with unknown observation maps are, and are not, identifiable, and demonstrate a general methodology (based on differential algebra) for assessing the identifiability of such models. This paves the way for applying classic differential equation workflows to the many real-world systems that contain such unknown maps.

Stability of Parallel Transport Convolution under Riemannian Metric Perturbations

Hongnian Huang, Andrew Stump

Geometric convolutions on Riemannian manifolds depend on the geometry of the underlying domain, making stability under metric perturbations a fundamental question in geometric deep learning. This paper studies this question for parallel transport convolution through methods from differential geometry and analysis. On a fixed compact manifold, we establish that, for a continuous input signal and a continuous reference kernel supported in a sufficiently small normal neighborhood, the convolution output depends continuously in $L^2$ on the Riemannian metric in the $C^2$-topology, under the specified localization and transport-path conventions. The analysis develops perturbation estimates for geodesics, parallel transport, Jacobi fields, and exponential maps, and uses these estimates to compare the transported kernels. A central difficulty is the cut locus, which obstructs uniform comparison of the transport construction over the entire manifold. We address this difficulty by combining uniform estimates away from the cut locus with integral bounds on neighborhoods of arbitrarily small volume, while also controlling the change in the Riemannian volume measure. The resulting qualitative stability theorem connects perturbation theory for Riemannian geometric constructions with the analysis of spatial convolution operators in geometric deep learning.

The sparse method can identify key discriminative features from high-dimensional data and suppress redundant information. Comprehensive numerical evaluations are conducted over multiple publicly available real-world benchmarks.The proposed methods demonstrate significant advantages in computational efficiency and scalability for large-scale problems, while achieving competitive classification accuracy on some datasets.

We introduce CurvDelta, a scalable framework that incorporates the preservation constraint into the parameterization of the model update. By analyzing the local geometry of the preservation constraint around the pre-adaptation model, CurvDelta constructs a low-curvature space in which parameter updates leave the preservation loss nearly invariant. We make its construction practical for LLMs through Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (K-FAC) and a factorized projection. For mathematical reasoning and code generation, our approaches achieve competitive downstream performance while better preserving general capabilities, offering a favorable acquisition--retention trade-off relative to the baselines. For knowledge unlearning, our main results on TOFU and MUSE show a better balance between targeted forgetting and knowledge retention than the baselines, with retention performance close to the from-scratch retraining reference.

Infinity-Wasserstein Distributionally Robust Principal Component Analysis: Exact Reformulation, Finite-Sample Guarantee, and Efficient Algorithm

Zhouxing Luo, Lei Wang, Xin Liu

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a fundamental and ubiquitous technique for dimension reduction and representation learning, yet its empirical formulation remains vulnerable to distributional uncertainty. While distributionally robust optimization (DRO) offers a natural remedy, existing order-2 Wasserstein models of PCA either preserve the nominal PCA subspace or lack an exact reformulation. This paper demonstrates how the infinity-Wasserstein distance can reconcile these two desirable properties. For the resulting DRO model of PCA, we derive an exact finite-dimensional reformulation and explicitly identify a worst-case distribution attaining the inner maximum. Crucially, the reformulated objective retains samplewise residual information beyond the empirical covariance matrix, revealing a mechanism through which distributional robustness can reshape the learned principal subspace. Under the mild assumption that the true distribution has a finite fourth moment, we further establish a uniform finite-sample performance guarantee for the proposed model. An iteratively reweighted majorization-minimization algorithm is also developed to solve the resulting nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization problem, with a rigorous analysis of its convergence properties. Numerical experiments substantiate the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithm and the practical effectiveness of the infinity-Wasserstein model in downstream applications.

Stable admissible neural classes and convergent physics-informed neural networks

Leevan Ling, Shu Ma, Yizhong Sun

Residual-based methods, including physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), physics-informed Kolmogorov--Arnold networks, and some physics-informed neural operator architectures, approximate PDE solutions by minimizing a discrete least-squares loss formed from the PDE and boundary residuals over a prescribed trial class. This structure is shared by classical least-squares Kansa collocation, whose kernel trial spaces admit an established convergence theory. For neural trial classes, approximation power alone does not ensure that discrete residuals control the continuous solution error. For linear elliptic Dirichlet problems, we extend the kernel-based collocation framework to neural trial classes through elliptic regularity, discrete stability, and consistency. Elliptic regularity supplies the PDE estimate relating solution error to continuous residuals. Discrete stability identifies admissible trial subclasses in which continuous residuals are controlled by a computable discrete loss. This step also quantifies the required number of collocation points. Furthermore, the stability analysis naturally demands a scale-dependent, physics-balanced boundary weight. For the constrained problem, consistency requires accurate comparison functions within the same admissible subclass, a requirement not supplied by neural approximation estimates alone. Specializing to multilayer perceptrons with tanh activations (tanh-MLPs), we construct admissible comparison networks and establish convergence for the constrained residual minimization problem. We further obtain conditional convergence of unconstrained physics-balanced PINNs based on tanh-MLPs under joint network and sampling refinement.

Regularized Score Centering

Hao Bai, Chenlu Ye, Xuanchang Zhang, Aviral Kumar, Tong Zhang

Asynchronous reinforcement learning increases training throughput, but data from stale policies and numerical mismatch between training and inference can destabilize learning. To stabilize off-policy learning, score centering (SC) removes the effect of reward baselines on the expected update. Our gradient diagnostics show less extreme gradients with SC than with importance sampling (IS), but SC can still fail when the learner moves far from the behavior policy that generated the data. To limit this policy mismatch, we introduce Regularized Score Centering (RSC), which adds a reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty to encourage the learner's next-token distribution to stay close to the behavior policy's next-token distribution. RSC works with or without truncated importance sampling (TIS), which caps token importance weights. Our theoretical analysis supports this approach, showing that KL regularization improves convergence guarantees. We evaluate dense and mixture-of-experts models on mathematical reasoning and agentic tasks, including online shopping, tool use, games, and fully asynchronous software engineering. RSC with TIS substantially improves sample efficiency across all agentic benchmarks, attaining higher training rewards with the same number of sampled responses. In final evaluations, it achieves top performance across all six agentic tasks, improving by 14.3% on AppWorld and reaching 9.9 times the strongest baseline's score on BabyAI.

Second scaling regime in high-dimensional function approximation and curse of dimensionality

Shukai Du, Thu Le, Samuel N. Stechmann

High-dimensional function approximation is often subject to the curse of dimensionality, commonly described by a hypercube scaling regime with a large expense of $R^d$ grid points, where $R$ denotes the side length of a hypercube in $d$ dimensions. Here we show that a second scaling regime, which we call the lattice ball scaling regime, also arises and is relevant for practical applications. In this regime, the expense scales as $d^{R^2}/(R^2!)$ and therefore grows polynomially with $d$. This regime arises without additional special assumptions of special types of function spaces or error norms. The lattice ball scaling regime occurs when $R^2\ll d$, due to the discrete nature of counting the expense as the number of lattice points inside a hypersphere. Here we investigate this regime, which arises from the classical problem of approximating high-dimensional functions from common spaces of analytic or $m$-times continuously differentiable functions using orthogonal series (spectral method) such as Fourier, Chebyshev, and Legendre polynomial series. For many applications, the expense in the lattice ball scaling regime can be substantially smaller than that of the hypercube scaling regime, making accurate approximations more accessible. Numerical illustrations include classifying images of handwritten digits from the MNIST dataset and learning the solution operators of partial differential equations, such as Burgers' equation and the radiative transfer equation. The potential for connections with artificial neural networks and other machine learning methods is also discussed.

Our plain zonotope method delivers competitive sound bounds nearly 300 times faster on average.

Kaiwen Shi, Zheyuan Zhang, Weixiang Sun, Han Bao, Colby Nelson, Yanfang Ye

When LLM agents pass intermediate judgments downstream without uncertainty, later decisions may treat tentative assumptions as facts. Effective uncertainty propagation must preserve information for correction and update inherited beliefs as new evidence arrives. Yet scalar scores omit information needed for revision, while detailed descriptions and reflection incur additional costs. We introduce Latent Uncertainty, a latent-space estimator that preserves uncertainty-relevant information without lengthy descriptions. Building on this, we propose LProp (Latent Uncertainty Propagation), which revises inherited answer distributions using latent representations and new evidence without additional trajectory sampling. We evaluate both methods across multiple-choice, yes/no, open-ended question answering, and mathematical reasoning tasks. Our approach outperforms baselines on most metrics, while LProp achieves the lowest mean inference-token usage in all eight propagation settings. Matched feature ablations further demonstrate that inherited latent content improves error recovery beyond answer probabilities or model capacity alone.

Stochastic Representation-Inspired Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Incompressible Fluid Flows

Zihao Guo, Zhongmin Qian

The effectiveness of neural networks in solving partial differential equations depends not only on their approximation capability but also on the suitability of their representations for the underlying solutions. Motivated by stochastic integral representations of incompressible fluid flows and the associated Biot--Savart kernel, we propose a Biot--Savart-informed (i.e. physics-informed) neural network (BS-PINN) for the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations. By extracting a rational profile from a regularized Biot--Savart kernel, we construct a smooth activation function with fixed and learnable kernel scales. Both variants retain the conventional velocity--pressure formulation and physics-informed training framework, allowing the influence of the activation function to be examined independently. We evaluate the method on five benchmarks, including Kovasznay flow, cylinder wake, three-dimensional Beltrami flow, Taylor--Green vortex, and double shear layer. Across 21 benchmark--architecture comparisons, both variants consistently reduce the combined velocity error relative to the conventional $\tanh$-based PINN, achieving mean relative error reductions of 31.7\% and 33.2\%, respectively. Improvements are also observed in reported pressure and vorticity errors. Notably, the double-shear-layer experiments demonstrate improved resolution of localized shear structures and, for BS-PINN-F, qualitatively clearer vortex-roll-up patterns in the long-time simulation. These findings highlight the potential of incorporating analytical flow structures into neural-network representations for incompressible flow simulations.

MCTS Is Counting the Wrong Thing: Learning Exploration Counts for LLM Reasoning

Xuan Zhang, Zhijian Zhou, Zicheng Hu, Yan Feng, Hongyan Hao, Yuan Qi

Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) has powered landmark successes in reinforcement learning, yet its gains in LLM reasoning can remain modest despite substantial search effort. We identify an overlooked bottleneck: what MCTS counts. Standard UCT assigns independent visit counts to textual branches, allowing different formulations of the same reasoning strategy to repeatedly receive exploration bonuses. Our theoretical analysis shows that this count fragmentation can impair exploration efficiency, as quantified by increased cumulative regret. To address this problem, we introduce Learned Pseudo-Count MCTS (LPC-MCTS), which changes only the exploration statistic in UCT. A lightweight Coin-Flipping Network head on the policy's hidden representations estimates representation coverage, enabling related branches to share exploration evidence. Across 5 mathematical-reasoning and 7 search benchmarks, LPC-MCTS consistently outperforms strong MCTS baselines. Holding each LPC-MCTS-trained policy fixed, replacing standard MCTS counts at inference improves average mean@16 accuracy across 5 mathematical benchmarks by 3.79 percentage points on Qwen2.5-7B and 2.71 points on Qwen3-4B. Applying LPC-MCTS during both training and inference yields gains of 4.96 and 4.37 points over standard MCTS, respectively. Together, our theory and experiments show that changing what MCTS counts can substantially improve its exploration efficiency and reasoning performance.

Large Language Models as Nonlinear Dynamical Systems: The Three Clocks of Depth, Training, and Generation

Maor Farid

Language models are dynamical systems. A transformer is iterated over its layers, over the steps of its training and over the tokens it generates, and on each of these three clocks it shows behavior that nonlinear dynamics has studied for a century: fixed points, bifurcations, synchronization, chaos and noise-driven escape from potential wells. The two communities rarely meet, so this behavior is often rediscovered under new names. We build a bridge between them. To describe, we condense about 200 papers into the Three-Clock Dictionary, which maps fifteen concepts of nonlinear dynamics onto each clock and marks every correspondence as proved, measured or conjectured, and we show each concept in a mechanical system and in a language model side by side. To explain, we derive reduced-order models: the softmax is a Boltzmann distribution, gradient descent an Euler step on a nonlinear spring, a loss spike a ball thrown out of a stiff valley, attention on the circle a Kuramoto model, and a repetition loop a well that deepens while it is occupied. We then test these explanations on open language models, on a laptop CPU. Escape from repetition loops in GPT-2 and Pythia follows the Arrhenius law with the logit gap as the barrier, and the barrier deepens and then saturates, so the temperature needed to leave a loop falls like $1/\ln N$ with the generation length $N$ and rises with model size. Coherent text lives in a narrow window of temperatures, which a small reasoning model shares at its recommended temperature. Training makes each Pythia layer more expansive, and a small transformer trains at a multi-mode edge of stability. The third step, solving problems of language models, is still ahead: controllers derived from the escape analysis remove loops but do not yet beat the best fixed temperature. We state twelve open problems where nonlinear dynamics could get there.

Efficient Variance-Aware Reinforcement Learning in Time-Inhomogeneous Linear MDPs

Qingyue Zhao, Qiwei Di, Jiafan He, Heyang Zhao, Kaixuan Ji, Quanquan Gu

We study variance-dependent reinforcement learning in episodic time-inhomogeneous linear Markov decision processes (MDPs) with feature dimension $d$, horizon $H$, and $K$ episodes. While recent works have established variance-dependent regret with $\tilde{O}(1)$ deterministic-regime overhead for linear mixture MDPs (Zhao et al., 2023) and worst-case nearly minimax optimal regret $\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{H^3 K} + d^7 H^8)$ for linear MDPs (He et al., 2023),, existing variance-aware analyses for linear MDPs (Li and Sun, 2023; Huang et al., 2023) rely on a coarse concentration step whose resulting regret guarantees retain a polynomial $\tilde{O}(K^{1/4})$ additive term even in deterministic environments. We propose VA-LSVI-UCB++, a computationally efficient algorithm that achieves the variance-dependent regret bound $\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{H\min\{\mathrm{Var}_K^*,\mathrm{Var}_K^π\}}+d^5H^4)$, where $\mathrm{Var}_K^*$ and $\mathrm{Var}_K^π$ denote the cumulative conditional variances of the optimal value function and the played policies' value functions along the realized trajectory. Our result adapts to the minimum of the two realized trajectory variances, recovers the worst-case minimax optimal leading term $\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{H^3 K})$, and collapses to $\tilde{O}(d^5 H^4)$ in deterministic environments, free of polynomial-in-$K$ dependency. At the core of our analysis is a simultaneous three-way reference-advantage decomposition for the optimistic, pessimistic, and squared value function estimators coupled with variance self-bounding and Freedman-type value-difference arguments, which tightens the pessimistic and second-moment confidence radii by a factor of $\tilde{O}(d H)$ and removes the inverse-variance-floor blowup.

Transferring an analysis of human brain organization to language models

Donghyeok Lee, Sangwon Yu, Sangkwon Park, Hye Youn Park, Munyoung Chang, Sungroh Yoon

During human cortical development, functional systems become increasingly differentiated. We asked whether improved reasoning in language models involves a comparable regional reorganization. Applying diffusion-map embedding, used to characterize cortical organization, to matched base and reasoning-trained models from three families, we found broadly preserved gradient profiles with localized changes within a contiguous layer span. Grafting weights from these spans into instruction-tuned hosts improved mathematical reasoning on average, with gains rising with region overlap, while retaining more previously solved problems than grafts elsewhere. Across training, the span formed gradually during the early rise in performance, and grafts from intermediate stages helped. Grafts shifted most hosts partly towards their donors' behaviour and sometimes improved on problems the donors failed; the same analysis located a transferable span in a medical model. Together, these findings identify a reasoning-related region whose weights improve reasoning with low interference in the host's existing mathematical competence.
