What the Machine Invented: Mining New Mathematical Instruments from OpenAI's October 2026 Release of AI-Generated Mathematics

Ying Xie

On 6 October 2026 OpenAI published an initial 722 AI-written mathematical manuscripts, organized into 372 problem families, in the public repository github.com/openai/math. The release includes claimed solutions to many prominent open problems. We approach this collection as a treasure mine of mathematical ideas. Our aim is to extract new lemmas, constructions, inequalities and proof mechanisms introduced in the source arguments, and to distinguish them from established tools and new combinations of those tools. We call these mathematical instruments. Extraction comes first; their validity, novelty and possible reuse then become subjects for further study. The resulting descriptions also help readers understand the selected papers without having to reconstruct every argument at the outset. We examine 38 families in two groups of 17 and 21, spanning number theory, geometry, analysis, combinatorics, complexity, algebra, operator algebras, topology and mathematical physics. Each entry records the claim, extracted instruments, antecedents and caveats. Eleven cross-cutting patterns connect the entries, and a scoreboard records formalization status and dependencies. Novelty assessments are provisional and sometimes mixed. Selected source comparisons correct mathematical statements, hypotheses and counts; they do not independently establish priority or certify the underlying proofs. No Lean development was compiled or kernel-checked for this survey. We also briefly describe a subsequent application of the signed restriction lemma from family 112 to circuit lower bounds at every fixed depth.

Recursive Gradient Retraction Methods for Stochastic Nonconvex Optimization on Stiefel Manifolds

Zhijie Yuan, Lei Sun, Ganzhao Yuan

Nonconvex optimization on Stiefel manifolds underlies many problems in machine learning and deep learning. However, existing stochastic Riemannian retraction methods often rely on stepsizes that require prior knowledge of the gradient Lipschitz constant, a prescribed iteration horizon, or restrictive batch-size choices. We develop {\sf RGR}, a recursive gradient retraction method for stochastic nonconvex optimization on Stiefel manifolds. Instead of recursively updating gradient estimators in changing tangent spaces, {\sf RGR} constructs them in the Euclidean space and subsequently maps them to tangent space, thereby avoiding vector transport. The method comprises three variants: {\sf RGR-SPIDER} for finite-sum optimization and {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} for online stochastic optimization. {\sf RGR-SPIDER} is Lipschitz-free and horizon-free, as its adaptive stepsize requires neither prior knowledge of the gradient Lipschitz constant nor a prescribed iteration horizon. {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} are mini-batch and horizon-free methods whose convergence guarantees do not rely on prescribed large batch sizes and whose parameters can be chosen without prior knowledge of the total iteration budget. We show that {\sf RGR-SPIDER} reaches an $\varepsilon$-critical point using $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})$ iterations and $\mathcal{O}(N+\sqrt{N}\varepsilon^{-2})$ component-gradient evaluations, where $N$ denotes the number of component functions. For the online setting, {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} achieve iteration complexities of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-3})$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-4})$, respectively. Experiments on principal component analysis, low-rank matrix completion, and orthogonal convolutional neural networks demonstrate faster empirical convergence than methods that construct recursive gradient estimators directly in Riemannian tangent spaces.

Neural Spline Flow Diffeomorphisms for Pullback Finite Element Methods on Complex Domains

Ioannis Kalogeris, Anaksagoras Spitas, Konstantinos Atzarakis, George Stavroulakis, Vissarion Papadopoulos

We propose a method for solving partial differential equations on complex domains by learning smooth, invertible mappings from a fixed reference domain using Neural Spline Flows (NSFs). Monotone rational-quadratic spline coupling layers ensure a strictly positive Jacobian determinant by construction, eliminating the need for Jacobian-sign penalties. A diffeomorphic pullback transfers the weak form of the PDE to the reference domain, enabling finite element analysis on reusable structured meshes. To generalize across shapes, a shared NSF is conditioned on low-dimensional latent codes learned through an auto-decoder framework; a subsequent encoder predicts these codes for unseen geometries in a single forward pass. For a given learned map, the pullback preserves the mathematical structure of the PDE and is exactly equivalent to solving on the domain represented by that map. Because the learned domain generally approximates the target geometry, geometric mapping error remains distinct from finite element discretization and quadrature errors. Numerical benchmarks show that sufficiently accurate learned mappings yield reliable finite element solutions. By avoiding repeated generation of unstructured meshes, the method addresses a bottleneck in simulation workflows. The fixed reference mesh also creates opportunities for efficient assembly, memory-aligned data access, and precomputed quadrature. The resulting framework assigns geometric complexity to the neural network while retaining conventional finite element discretization and convergence properties on the learned domain, with overall accuracy also governed by geometric fidelity.

Boundary Control of a Modified Timoshenko-Ehrenfest Beam via Reduced-Order Reinforcement Learning

Leonardo R S Rodrigues

In this paper, we study the active vibration control of an elastic beam using reinforcement learning (RL). The physical system is modeled by a modified Timoshenko-Ehrenfest type linear partial differential equation with simply supported boundary conditions. The dynamics are approximated by a reduced-order model (ROM) obtained through eigenvalue expansion and truncated Galerkin projection. Control is applied through boundary actuation. The resulting finite-dimensional system is formulated as a continuous-time Markov decision process and solved numerically using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. All modeling steps, from physical formulation to temporal discretization and control design, are explicitly described. A systematic comparison is performed between the controller learned via PPO and the classical linear quadratic (LQR) regulator. The analysis highlights the strengths and limitations of each approach, both from a theoretical and practical point of view, with particular emphasis on energy dissipation mechanisms and actuator constraints.

When Does Momentum Help in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards?

Kaifei Wang, Zikang Shan, Liwei Wang, Yinyu Ye, Han Zhong

Momentum is a classical tool for accelerating convex optimization and a standard component of modern large language model training. Yet recent empirical work has questioned the usefulness of first-moment momentum in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). We study this question through a bandit formulation of RLVR with bias-corrected exponential moving average (EMA) momentum, where the EMA coefficient $β$ controls how quickly the weights on past gradients decay. When policy gradients are evaluated exactly, we prove that increasing $β$ within a range that prevents overshoot strictly accelerates optimization. When gradients are estimated from sampled responses, a positive expected gain can be obscured by sampling fluctuations. We show that as the learning rate $η$ decreases, the expected gain with fixed $β$ vanishes faster than these fluctuations, whereas increasing $β$ to keep $η/(1-β)$ within the safe regime preserves a nonvanishing gain that eventually dominates them. Bandit simulations quantitatively validate these predictions. RLVR experiments on Qwen3-4B-Base using SGD with EMA and AdamW support our principle of choosing larger coefficients in practical LLM post-training. On Qwen2.5-32B with AdamW, simply increasing $β_1$ improves mean validation accuracy by nearly $2$ percentage points over the conventional choice, demonstrating the principle's effectiveness at a larger model scale.

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is widely used in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards for language-model reasoning. While \emph{value evolution along trajectory} characterizes relative token-level credit, yet standard critics often fail to track it under sparse terminal rewards. To model this evolution, we analyze the relation between successive prefix values and show that they admit a Bayesian decomposition with additive updates in log-odds space. We use this additive structure to construct Tracking-Value PPO (\method), a cumulative value parameterization that sums position-wise scalar outputs into value logits. Gains extend to Qwen3-4B, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen3.5-9B and to countdown, and scientific coding tasks. Further analysis shows more accurate tracking of value evolution and more reliable advantage estimates. Our code is publicly available in https://github.com/LINs-lab/TPPO.

From Global to Tail Convergence: Dynamics of $α$-Stable Score-Based Generative Models

Ianis Hammani, Claire Boyer

Score-based generative models driven by $α$-stable L{é}vy processes have been proposed as an alternative to Brownian diffusion models, with empirical evidence suggesting improved sampling and better modeling of heavy-tailed data. Yet theoretical guarantees for these models remain limited. We develop a non-asymptotic convergence analysis for scale-preserving $α$-stable generative dynamics, covering both compactly supported and power-law targets. In p-Wasserstein distance, we quantify the effects of early stopping, initialization, score approximation, and time discretization, and show exponential decay of the initialization error. A comparison with Brownian dynamics reveals that global Wasserstein convergence alone does not fundamentally distinguish the two models: both initialization errors decay exponentially with the forward horizon on the common class of heavy-tailed targets. A sharp separation emerges instead in the tails. At any positive generation time, stable dynamics produces power-law tails with index $α$, whereas Brownian dynamics remains light-tailed. For a target with tail index $ν$, asymptotically correct extreme-quantile scaling is possible precisely when $α$=$ν$. These results identify tail-index matching as a principled mechanism behind the ability of stable generative models to capture extreme events.

Exact Topological Compliance: Generating Persistence-Equivalent Graphs

Mattie Ji, Indradyumna Roy, Vikas Garg

Persistent homology summarizes the topological evolution of a graph as persistence diagrams (PDs). Generating graphs whose topology matches a target descriptor is increasingly important, yet existing topology-aware generative models rely on soft regularization, producing graphs whose PDs may only be approximately similar. We initiate a principled investigation of the generation problem: produce graphs that realize a target PD exactly. We propose two approaches guided by the implicit local and global constraints a target PD imposes. The local approach builds degree-based PD-equivalent graphs iteratively, and we prove that every output realizes the target PD and that every degree-based PD-equivalent graph is reachable. However, as shown thereafter, local generation necessarily trades off between sample diversity and reaching infeasible states. We therefore recast generation as a global constraint satisfaction problem with a complete encoding solvable by modern constraint programming solvers. This is applicable to any vertex-based permutation equivariant filtration scheme and is minimal under the degree filtration. Experiments on four standard graph generation benchmarks confirm that both methods realize the target PD exactly. Together, our methods provide a principled treatment of strict topology-compliant graph generation. Code is available at https://github.com/Aalto-QuML/ExactCompliance.

LeanPRM: Lean-Grounded Process Supervision for Natural Language Proof Verification

Rodney Lafuente-Mercado, Salman Rahman, Jingyan Shen, James Shiffer, Saadia Gabriel, Pavel Izmailov

AI systems now write long and difficult mathematical proofs, and checking their step-by-step validity is becoming a bottleneck. A learned verifier could check proofs cheaply, but training one requires a correctness label on every step, and such labels are hard to obtain for open-ended proofs. Existing methods take them from human annotators, which is costly, or derive them from final answers, which proofs do not have, and formalizing each proof in Lean requires expert effort. We introduce LeanPRM, a framework that uses the Lean proof assistant to create step-level labels for natural-language proofs. A model writes a natural-language proof together with a paired formal proof, without feedback from Lean. Lean then labels every step, and a second model discards failures caused only by the formal syntax, so that the negative labels mark errors in the mathematics. LeanPRM contains 10,634 proofs with 152K labeled steps and no human annotation. A Qwen3-4B verifier trained on LeanPRM raises exact step-label accuracy on a held-out set of 200 proofs (LeanPRM-Bench) from 48.2 to 74.7, as opposed to 60.3 when the same base model is trained on PRM800K. It also selects better proofs in best-of-N selection on IMO-level problems, and outperforms the PRM800K-trained verifier on three of four external verification benchmarks. Training on LeanPRM improves verifiers at every size from 0.6B to 8B parameters on LeanPRM-Bench, Hard2Verify and IMO-GradingBench. Given the labels need no human annotation, LeanPRM can grow with the supply of formal statements, offering a path to step-level feedback on AI-generated proofs that are hard for humans to check.

Because each approach has a distinct failure mode, a growing body of work combines them so that a session key remains secure if either component holds. We first establish the deployed baseline, hybrid classical-plus-PQC key exchange, against which QKD-augmented designs must be judged. We examine key-combiner constructions in light of the KEM-combiner literature and of NIST, ETSI, and ITU-T guidance, and map the security arguments for hybrid PQC-QKD protocols onto a common grid of security model, assumptions, permitted compromise, established guarantee, and limitations. We synthesise deployment evidence from metropolitan and national QKD networks, distinguishing the few testbeds that have run PQC in the key path from pure-QKD deployments, and summarise the standards and policy landscape, including the positions of national security agencies that recommend against relying on QKD alone. We close with open problems: authentication of the QKD classical channel, shared-infrastructure threat models, device certification versus formal proofs, formal verification of hybrid handshakes, and the lack of peer-reviewed deployment economics.
