Functional Subnetwork Localization for Efficient Continual Learning in Large Language Models

Shuoran Jiang, Wentao Zhu, Yang Xiang, Dongfeng Li, Yunan Zhang, Mengchen Zhao

Biological intelligence continually adapts to new information while retaining prior skills through localized plasticity. In contrast, continual learning in large language models (LLMs) remains vulnerable to catastrophic forgetting. A promising direction is to identify functionally specialized subnetworks within LLMs that enable targeted and resource-efficient adaptation. Using gradient-attribution profiling, we localize cognition-associated functional subnetworks exhibiting extremely low mutual overlap. Building on this finding, we propose Function-isolated Memory-Efficient Optimization (CiMEO), a continual learning framework that routes each training example to its corresponding functional subnetwork, restricting parameter updates and optimizer states to selected regions. Across 1.4 million sequential instructions, CiMEO achieves the highest average performance among the compared methods on five generative benchmarks spanning programming, examination-style tasks, and mathematical reasoning, while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Subspace-overlap and principal-angle analyses further indicate that localized updates help preserve pre-trained representations, highlighting functional subnetwork localization as a practical approach to efficient and stable continual learning in LLMs.

Recursive Gradient Retraction Methods for Stochastic Nonconvex Optimization on Stiefel Manifolds

Zhijie Yuan, Lei Sun, Ganzhao Yuan

Nonconvex optimization on Stiefel manifolds underlies many problems in machine learning and deep learning. However, existing stochastic Riemannian retraction methods often rely on stepsizes that require prior knowledge of the gradient Lipschitz constant, a prescribed iteration horizon, or restrictive batch-size choices. We develop {\sf RGR}, a recursive gradient retraction method for stochastic nonconvex optimization on Stiefel manifolds. Instead of recursively updating gradient estimators in changing tangent spaces, {\sf RGR} constructs them in the Euclidean space and subsequently maps them to tangent space, thereby avoiding vector transport. The method comprises three variants: {\sf RGR-SPIDER} for finite-sum optimization and {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} for online stochastic optimization. {\sf RGR-SPIDER} is Lipschitz-free and horizon-free, as its adaptive stepsize requires neither prior knowledge of the gradient Lipschitz constant nor a prescribed iteration horizon. {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} are mini-batch and horizon-free methods whose convergence guarantees do not rely on prescribed large batch sizes and whose parameters can be chosen without prior knowledge of the total iteration budget. We show that {\sf RGR-SPIDER} reaches an $\varepsilon$-critical point using $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})$ iterations and $\mathcal{O}(N+\sqrt{N}\varepsilon^{-2})$ component-gradient evaluations, where $N$ denotes the number of component functions. For the online setting, {\sf RGR-SRM} and {\sf RGR-EMA} achieve iteration complexities of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-3})$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-4})$, respectively. Experiments on principal component analysis, low-rank matrix completion, and orthogonal convolutional neural networks demonstrate faster empirical convergence than methods that construct recursive gradient estimators directly in Riemannian tangent spaces.

Bias Control and Injection for Multi-Agent Aggregation

Sungjae Lee, Soheil Abbasloo, Jabin Koo, Jungseul Ok

Multi-agent reasoning with large language models (LLMs) raises a central question for collective decision-making: when agents disagree, whose decision should be trusted? Resolving such disagreements is difficult because agents' decisions are susceptible to non-semantic biases such as majority pressure and response-style preferences, which can obscure the quality of the underlying reasoning. Our analysis shows how bias can be both mitigated and exploited: bias control improves initial answer selection, while bias injection elicits decision instability that serves as a reliability signal. Building on these findings, we introduce a test-time aggregation framework that requires neither validation data nor prior knowledge of relative model strength. The framework implements bias control by presenting one style-normalized reasoning trace per distinct answer, and bias injection by placing each aggregating agent's initially selected answer in the minority. Overall, our framework achieves higher average accuracy and better token efficiency than competing non-oracle aggregation methods across three mathematical reasoning benchmarks and four heterogeneous LLM combinations. It also improves final-answer accuracy when applied to multi-agent debate and Mixture-of-Agents, without modifying their interaction or synthesis processes.

Neural Spline Flow Diffeomorphisms for Pullback Finite Element Methods on Complex Domains

Ioannis Kalogeris, Anaksagoras Spitas, Konstantinos Atzarakis, George Stavroulakis, Vissarion Papadopoulos

We propose a method for solving partial differential equations on complex domains by learning smooth, invertible mappings from a fixed reference domain using Neural Spline Flows (NSFs). Monotone rational-quadratic spline coupling layers ensure a strictly positive Jacobian determinant by construction, eliminating the need for Jacobian-sign penalties. A diffeomorphic pullback transfers the weak form of the PDE to the reference domain, enabling finite element analysis on reusable structured meshes. To generalize across shapes, a shared NSF is conditioned on low-dimensional latent codes learned through an auto-decoder framework; a subsequent encoder predicts these codes for unseen geometries in a single forward pass. For a given learned map, the pullback preserves the mathematical structure of the PDE and is exactly equivalent to solving on the domain represented by that map. Because the learned domain generally approximates the target geometry, geometric mapping error remains distinct from finite element discretization and quadrature errors. Numerical benchmarks show that sufficiently accurate learned mappings yield reliable finite element solutions. By avoiding repeated generation of unstructured meshes, the method addresses a bottleneck in simulation workflows. The fixed reference mesh also creates opportunities for efficient assembly, memory-aligned data access, and precomputed quadrature. The resulting framework assigns geometric complexity to the neural network while retaining conventional finite element discretization and convergence properties on the learned domain, with overall accuracy also governed by geometric fidelity.

Gibbs-Gap Policy Optimization for Off-Policy RL with Verifiable Rewards

Xuanchang Zhang, Tong Zhang

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards often trains language models on responses generated by a different, possibly stale behavior policy. Correcting this mismatch with learner-to-behavior importance weights can introduce high variance for long responses. We introduce Gibbs-Gap Policy Optimization (GGPO), which minimizes a log-mean-exp contrast of KL-adjusted rewards among responses to the same prompt, without learner-to-behavior importance weights. We derive GGPO from KL-regularized policy optimization: on policy, its population loss is exactly the KL-regularized optimality gap, and under suitable coverage the learner's single-response expected reward is at least the behavior policy's best-of-$K$ reward, minus terms for the raw finite-group loss, regularization, and finite training data. We characterize reference refresh through exact Gibbs-target composition and a finite-data final-policy reward bound under local reference control and tokenwise behavior mismatch. This refresh is empirically important: in an ablation, fixed-reference GGPO scores $32.96$ on the five-benchmark problem-weighted average, versus $46.58$ with the default refresh schedule. Experiments on mathematical reasoning further show that GGPO outperforms GRPO and AGRO under training-inference mismatch and on fixed offline data, while maintaining its performance as controlled policy staleness grows.

When is a better LLM worth paying for? Stochastic Optimization with Switching Mixed-Fidelity Oracles

Swati Gupta, Tianjiao Li

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used upstream of decision-making to clean data, detect label errors, and reconstruct missing values. Yet LLM-assisted data curation creates a new operational tradeoff: higher-fidelity models and richer prompts can be substantially more expensive, while the value of this additional fidelity depends on the downstream optimization accuracy. This raises the central question of this work: when should one pay for a better LLM, given a specific downstream task at hand? We study this question through stochastic optimization with mixed-fidelity first-order oracles, which can have systematic bias and state-dependent variance. We develop two cost-aware algorithmic frameworks that allow adaptive switching between stochastic oracles. With known bias, the first meta-algorithm applies a restarted, cost-aware policy that adaptively selects oracle fidelity across optimization stages. Its cumulative cost is no larger than that of the best fixed oracle in hindsight and can be substantially smaller. With unknown bias, we develop a budgeted certification scheme that uses occasional trusted-gradient evaluations to certify the optimization progress and eliminate unsuccessful oracle sources, and show an explicit tradeoff between certification cost and delayed detection of biased sources. We provide concrete realizations of these meta-algorithms by applying biased stochastic gradient extrapolation (SGE) and recursive regularization under state-dependent noise as subroutines. Synthetic and LLM-completion experiments illustrate the qualitative switching behavior predicted by the theory: the adaptive policy skips several expensive LLM oracles and substantially reduces cumulative cost relative to fixed high-fidelity alternatives while achieving comparable accuracy.

When Does Momentum Help in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards?

Kaifei Wang, Zikang Shan, Liwei Wang, Yinyu Ye, Han Zhong

Momentum is a classical tool for accelerating convex optimization and a standard component of modern large language model training. Yet recent empirical work has questioned the usefulness of first-moment momentum in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). We study this question through a bandit formulation of RLVR with bias-corrected exponential moving average (EMA) momentum, where the EMA coefficient $β$ controls how quickly the weights on past gradients decay. When policy gradients are evaluated exactly, we prove that increasing $β$ within a range that prevents overshoot strictly accelerates optimization. When gradients are estimated from sampled responses, a positive expected gain can be obscured by sampling fluctuations. We show that as the learning rate $η$ decreases, the expected gain with fixed $β$ vanishes faster than these fluctuations, whereas increasing $β$ to keep $η/(1-β)$ within the safe regime preserves a nonvanishing gain that eventually dominates them. Bandit simulations quantitatively validate these predictions. RLVR experiments on Qwen3-4B-Base using SGD with EMA and AdamW support our principle of choosing larger coefficients in practical LLM post-training. On Qwen2.5-32B with AdamW, simply increasing $β_1$ improves mean validation accuracy by nearly $2$ percentage points over the conventional choice, demonstrating the principle's effectiveness at a larger model scale.

We present Euler, a human-centered mathematical research system built around a shared, structured representation of the research process. We illustrate Euler's capabilities through three mathematical research case studies and describe the implemented system and its software validation.

Learning Warm Starts for the Simplex Method from Measured Solver Cost

Felix Parker, Danielle A. Ripsman, Emmett Springer, Kimia Ghobadi

Learned warm starts for the simplex method build starting bases using predictions of which variables will be basic at optimality. Prior work has shown that starting bases predicted by graph neural networks can sharply reduce iteration counts, but the time saved may fall well short of the iterations saved. On 25 classes of linear programs (LPs), we find that a strong supervised basis predictor removes two thirds of the dual simplex's iterations but only a third of its time. Two effects explain the gap: the iterations removed by a good start tend to be cheap, and closeness to an optimal basis does not rank starts by cost. We prove that two starts with equal overlap with an optimal basis and equal pivot counts can differ in linear-algebra work by a factor that grows with the square root of the number of rows. Empirically, across the starts of one LP, the pivot count explains 71% of the variance of log solve time, while the CPU instruction count explains 98.5%. We introduce CostStart, which uses supervised prediction for the basis and trains a small policy on measured instruction counts to choose, per LP, how many predicted columns to use, which algorithm and pricing rule to run, and whether to warm-start at all. On unseen LPs of the training classes, including LPs four times the training size, CostStart roughly halves the solve time relative to the solver's default start and is 1.4 to 2.2 times as fast as two published methods retrained on the same data.

Why Shallow Neural Networks Learn Sinusoidal Components First: An Orthogonal Polynomial Dynamics Framework

Yanyang Xiao

Neural networks often learn low-frequency components before high-frequency ones --- a phenomenon called spectral bias or termed F-principle. Many numerical studies have demonstrated this phenomenon, and theoretical studies have sought to explain it. However, several goals remain insufficiently addressed: (1) explaining spectral bias asymptotically, including its origin and why the preferred components are generally sinusoidal; (2) extending the explanation to general activation functions, such as sigmoid and ReLU; (3) making weak assumptions about the data distribution; (4) avoiding aliasing effects, such as those arising in finite-domain Fourier analysis; and (5) developing a theoretical framework applicable to arbitrary networks. We address these goals theoretically by analyzing the eigenvalue decay and eigenvector shapes of the dynamics matrix H, which arises when we expand the network output as a general Fourier series (GFS) in an orthogonal polynomial basis and express the gradient-descent dynamics of the GFS coefficients as ordinary differential equations. The graded structure of H enables an intuitive explanation of spectral bias, while orthogonal polynomial theory helps us analyze how learning bias depends on the activation function and, potentially, the network architecture.

Two Sample Sizes in Test-Time LLM Ensembling: Buffered ERM and Minimax Theory

Zhi Zhang, Arash A. Amini

Test-time scaling can improve the performance of large language models by generating multiple answers to a question and aggregating them. Weighted ensembles extend this idea by drawing answers from several models, with the weights determining how often each model is used. Learning these weights from a training dataset involves two sample sizes: $Q$ labeled questions and $N$ generations per model and question. We study their interplay through the clean risk, the population error of the ensemble's infinite-generation voting rule. Under a margin condition with exponent $β$, we show that empirical risk minimization (ERM) with a calibrated margin buffer achieves excess risk of order $Q^{-1/2}+N^{-β/2}$, up to logarithmic factors, for fixed numbers of models and candidate answers. To investigate the optimality of this dependence on $Q$ and $N$, we analyze a simplified submodel with two models and two possible answers per question. We characterize its minimax excess risk up to constants by a Hellinger modulus for a binomial-mixture experiment. Polynomial approximation and spectral analysis reveal three regimes. When questions are relatively scarce, the $Q^{-1/2}$ term governs learning and the general buffered ERM is optimal. In an intermediate regime, pooling generation counts across questions improves upon estimating each answer distribution separately, with only logarithmic gains from additional questions. With sufficiently many questions, the minimax excess risk reaches an $N^{-β}$ identification floor that persists even with infinitely many questions. The submodel's lower bounds also apply to general ensembles, matching the buffered-ERM upper bound up to logarithmic factors when questions are scarce and in parts of the intermediate regime. Questions remain about the transitions between regimes and the construction of matching pooled estimators for general ensembles.

Minimizing Age of Incorrect Information in Pull-Based Remote Estimation of a Markov Source

Ismail Cosandal, Sennur Ulukus, Nail Akar

In this paper, we consider a discrete-time pull-based remote estimation system involving an information source and a remote monitor where the information source is a finite-state irreducible discrete-time Markov chain (DTMC) process. In this system, the remote monitor sends a pull request towards the source which responds by sampling its process so as to transmit the sampled values towards the monitor through a delay channel, encapsulated in information packets. We consider i) zero delay (ZD) and ii) unit delay (UD) channels, where the packet transmission times are zero and one slot, respectively. For remote estimation, we use the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator, and for quantifying its performance, we use the mean age of incorrect information (MAoII) which is a recently proposed freshness and mismatch metric that penalizes an incorrect estimation along with its duration. Our goal is to obtain pull policies so as to minimize MAoII under a long-run sampling rate constraint. For the ZD model, we propose a semi-Markov decision process (SMDP) formulation of the problem, which allows us to find the optimum observation-dependent pull policy. On the other hand, the UD channel prevents the monitor from possessing perfect information on the actual AoII process at any given time, and the SMDP formulation of the ZD channel cannot readily be used for the UD model. For the system with the UD channel, we first provide a numerical algorithm to calculate the MAoII and the average sampling rate for a given observation-dependent pull policy. For the optimization of the underlying remote estimation system, we use the joint distribution of the age and source processes to be obtained from the history of all observations, termed as belief. Subsequently, we propose a belief-dependent policy obtained via the belief-MDP formulation and a constrained deep reinforcement learning (CDRL) algorithm.

The Tilted Sampling Problem: Optimism, Posterior Sampling, and Logarithmic Regret

Mohammad Pedramfar, Siamak Ravanbakhsh

Many modern learning systems are used not to select a single best action, but to sample diverse high-quality outputs. Examples include entropy-regularized reinforcement learning, RLHF, and reward-guided generative modeling. Motivated by these settings, we study tilted sampling: an online learning problem in which the goal is to sample from the Gibbs distribution induced by an unknown reward function. We define sampling regret as the temperature-normalized cumulative KL divergence between the learner's distribution and the Gibbs target, corresponding to the suboptimality gap in entropy-regularized reward maximization. Our framework generalizes recent sampling-based and KL-regularized formulations in bandits and reinforcement learning. Our main contribution is a general regret framework that captures the different uncertainty structure of tilted sampling and classical regret minimization. While standard regret scales as $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})$ due to first-order uncertainty, sampling regret is governed by squared confidence widths, yielding logarithmic-in-$T$ regret in standard settings at fixed temperature. We develop sampling analogues of UCB and Thompson sampling (UCS and TS$^2$) and provide unified regret decompositions that explain and extend recent logarithmic guarantees, including the first Bayesian sampling-regret decomposition based on new bounds for posterior-random Gibbs distributions. Our results show that learning to sample from a soft-optimal distribution is statistically distinct from learning to identify a hard optimum.

Adaptive Randomized Neural Networks for PDEs: Structure-Aware Feature Redistribution and Residual-Driven Block Enrichment

You Yang, Fei Wang

Randomized neural networks retain an output-linear least-squares solve, but globally sampled random features may fail to capture the spatial and scale structure of PDE solutions. We propose SFRBE--RaNN, a two-stage adaptive trial-space construction. SFR redistributes a fixed feature budget according to regional indicator masses and adapts regional weight scales through low-dimensional residual-based optimization. RBE constructs nonredundant enrichments by screening multiple activation families, removing represented response directions, and selecting the smallest admissible prefix. For solution-dependent operators, candidates are screened with a frozen operator and accepted only after nonlinear residual verification. For fixed linear problems, we establish an exact discrete residual-reduction identity and a conditional solution-error estimate. Building on this identity, we combine safeguarded SFR with RBE capture factors to derive a finite-horizon error bound involving baseline best approximation and empirical consistency, together with a conditional random-search success-probability bound. Numerical experiments on localized, multiscale, and nonlinear space--time problems demonstrate the method's effectiveness.

IneqAgent: Agentic Inequality Proving in Lean with Symbolic Computation

Gaolei He, Jinhui Li, Ruobing Zuo, Xia Zeng, Zhengfeng Yang

Formal inequality proving requires mathematical reasoning and exact computation, often connected through intermediate lemmas and goal reformulations. We introduce IneqAgent, an agentic framework combining LLM reasoning with symbolic computation for inequality proving in Lean4. Lemma-level Monte Carlo Tree Search coordinates lemma construction, goal reformulation, and symbolic backend selection. Complementary sum-of-squares, Bernstein, and successive difference-substitution backends produce exact certificates for local Lean proof generation. The agent integrates these proofs with LLM-generated steps and uses Lean feedback to construct complete proofs checked against the original theorem statements. On four benchmarks, IneqAgent with DeepSeek-V4-Flash solves 175 of 186 problems (94.09%), achieving the highest overall success rate among the evaluated methods. Across four backbones, it achieves higher success rates at low LLM inference cost thresholds than Direct Agent using the same backbone, with lower mean inference costs over each method's successful runs. We also obtain Lean-verified strengthenings of Dittert's conjecture in dimensions four and five, and certify the Holens--Djokovic $(4,4)$ inequality, implying Merris's conjecture in dimension four.

A Rate Transfer Method: Convergence Rates in Stochastic Gradient Descent via Stochastic Calculus

Neil Walton

We develop a rate-transfer method that converts finite-time Lyapunov estimates for an associated stochastic differential equation (SDE) into finite-time convergence bounds for stochastic gradient algorithms. Under generic moment and smoothness conditions, the convergence analysis of the stochastic gradient method reduces to a calculus argument verifying a Lyapunov generator inequality for the SDE. The method recovers established rates for classical stochastic gradient methods and provides a way to establish convergence rates for new optimization algorithms through calculus arguments applied to their associated SDEs.

Towards Learning-Guided Discovery of Extremal Geometric Constructions: A PatternBoost Study

Deepak Ajwani, Paula Carroll, Rishikesh Gajjala, Ananta Manoranjan, Ryan O'Connor, Rajiv Raman

Extremal constructions play a central role in the design and analysis of algorithms. Integrality-gap instances expose the limitations of relaxations, while hard configurations reveal structural sources of computational difficulty. Such constructions, however, are still typically discovered through a slow, manual process. This paper investigates how machine learning can accelerate their discovery to support algorithm design and analysis. We build on PatternBoost, a framework alternating local search with generation from a trained transformer, using its Axplorer implementation to investigate three problems: integrality gaps for maximum independent set of rectangles, integrality gaps for stabbing unit squares with axis-parallel lines, and hard instances for recursive guillotine cutting. By exploring candidate representations, local search operations, and surrogate models used to rank candidates before exact verification, we carefully adapt the framework to these combinatorial geometry problems.While naive representations and search strategies fail to find non-trivial instances, our tailored adaptation discovers numerous non-trivial constructions. For stabbing unit squares, the method reaches a gap of $20/13\approx 1.54$, improving on the literature's best known lower bound of $3/2$. Purely automated search converges rapidly and then plateaus, prompting the question of what is required to push the bound higher still. We find that initial instance diversity plays an important role: with modest expert input via a structured staircase construction, the system reaches an improved integrality gap of $14/9\approx 1.555$. These results demonstrate that machine learning and domain expertise can be combined to discover new extremal constructions that advance algorithmic lower bounds.

Learning Rational Strategies for Temporal Objectives in Multi-Agent Systems

Omar Adalat, Edwin Hamel-De le Court, Francesco Belardinelli

We study rational synthesis for stochastic multi-agent systems with unknown dynamics, where each agent is equipped with its own probabilistic temporal objective specified using Probabilistic Linear Temporal Logic on Finite Traces ($\mathsf{PLTL}_f}$). We impose solution concepts (e.g., Nash equilibrium, subgame-perfect equilibrium) as constraints on the joint strategy profile, using payoffs derived from each agent's probability of satisfying its temporal objective. In particular, we formulate per-agent rational synthesis as finding a strategy profile -- if one exists -- such that (i) the designated agent's probabilistic temporal constraint is satisfied, and (ii) the profile represents a stable solution under a chosen solution concept. We give conditional convergence guarantees for tabular Nash-Q learning, certify satisfaction with simultaneous finite-sample confidence bounds, and assess strategic stability with approximate best-response checking. Our approach operates in a stochastic \emph{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning} (MARL) environment, where model-free learning algorithms attempt to attain extremal satisfaction probabilities for temporal objectives automatically compiled into reward automata. Our approach is empirically validated using 8 general-sum concurrent stochastic games.

VASPO: Self-Distillation with Privileged Value Estimation

Fei Wu, Zhenrong Zhang, Jianshu Zhang, Jun Du

On-policy self-distillation provides dense supervision for reasoning models via a privileged self-teacher. However, this supervision reflects local token preferences rather than the expected return of an unguided student continuation. Recent methods combine this signal with GRPO. They use GRPO for update directions and the teacher for signal scaling. However, we demonstrate a more effective integration within PPO. Specifically, we identify complementary positional dynamics: privileged information enhances a critic's value-prediction accuracy toward the response's end, whereas self-teacher guidance is strong initially, drops mid-generation, and rebounds finally. These insights motivate Value-Augmented Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (VASPO), wherein a self-teacher and critic evaluate identical privileged information at student-generated prefixes. The critic computes token-level PPO advantages from predicted continuation returns, while the self-teacher modulates selected positive advantages via bounded probability ratios. Across six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, VASPO improves mean accuracy over PPO by 2.59 and 1.81 percentage points on Qwen3-4B and OLMo3-7B variants, respectively, and achieves the highest mean accuracy among evaluated methods on Knights and Knaves. Our code is available at https://github.com/cii030/VASPO.
