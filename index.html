Multifidelity Operator Inference for Non-intrusive Model Reduction under Data Scarcity

Vivian Zhang, Tomoki Koike, Atticus Rex, Elizabeth Qian

Many-query analyses, in which a computational model is repeatedly evaluated, play an important role in uncertainty quantification and optimization for engineering design. However, many-query analyses can be prohibitively expensive when considering high-fidelity numerical models, especially those governed by partial differential equations (PDEs). Surrogate modeling replaces costly simulations with cheap approximations, enabling practical uncertainty quantification and design workflows. Machine learning (ML) approaches to surrogate modeling have gained significant traction in the development of surrogate models due to their non-intrusive nature; they do not require access to the underlying simulation code. The Operator Inference (OpInf) approach, in particular, fits reduced operators to state and corresponding time derivative data. However, high-fidelity training data is scarce due to limited computational budgets, and OpInf models trained on such scarce data often exhibit high variance, a critical liability in high-risk engineering design. We propose Multifidelity Operator Inference (MF-OpInf), which reduces this variance by supplementing scarce high-fidelity snapshots with abundant low-fidelity data drawn from coarser discretizations of the same problem. MF-OpInf handles data across varying fidelity levels by utilizing a multifidelity linear regression framework to estimate the reduced operators. Numerical experiments on model PDE problems demonstrate that MF-OpInf predicts PDE solution fields with higher accuracy compared to the high-fidelity-only OpInf with the same computational budget.

Block-Sparse Gauss-Newton Optimization of Finite Basis Physics-Informed Neural Networks: Sparse Automatic Differentiation Approach

Denis Korolev, Adrian Hill, Jeeeun Lee

In this work, we develop an efficient Gauss-Newton optimization approach for finite basis physics-informed neural networks (FBPINNs). The key ingredient is the overlapping domain decomposition architecture of FBPINNs, which localizes the influence of each subnetwork and induces block sparsity in both the residual Jacobian and the corresponding Gauss-Newton matrix. We characterize this structure and exploit it through sparse automatic differentiation, using graph coloring to partition structurally orthogonal Jacobian columns or rows. This reduces differentiation costs and memory requirements while preserving all structurally nonzero derivative information. The resulting compressed Jacobian representations allow us to assemble the Gauss-Newton system directly in block-sparse form, enabling the use of sparse direct solvers and preconditioned iterative methods. In this way, the otherwise expensive second-order optimization problem is reduced to scalable sparse linear algebra. We demonstrate the approach on multiscale PDEs, where accurate approximation requires rich neural network parametrizations and large numbers of collocation points. Numerical experiments show that our block-sparse Gauss-Newton method attains high accuracy and substantially accelerates optimization relative to first-order baselines while remaining computationally tractable for large problem sizes.

Catch It Where It Happens: Real Time Error Correction During LLM Reasoning

Rotem Elimelech, Michael Shalyt, Ido Kaminer

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly trusted with mathematical, scientific and engineering work, yet their mistakes are often confidently written and hard to spot. A single wrong step tends to propagate, making the model waste inference tokens following a false premise. Existing safeguards either check only the final answer and restart from scratch, or rely on a verifier LLM that competes for GPU capacity and slows down response time. We propose online, deterministic verification: many kinds of reasoning steps can be validated by code, so a step can be verified in real time, as it is written, without any model call or GPU use. Whenever a check fails, we point out the mistake and let the model continue from the same spot. We demonstrate this approach on symbolic mathematics, where derivations already contain explicit expressions that a computer algebra system can check in milliseconds. Tested on the ASyMoB benchmark with three open-weights models, our online validation improves accuracy by 1.4-5.6 percentage points, corresponding to a 1.8-14.9% net reduction in incorrect answers, while increasing token usage by only 6-7%. Because the verifier is independent of the model, the approach complements progress in LLMs rather than competing with it: every new model can benefit immediately, and the benefit grows as deterministic checkers extend to more kinds of reasoning steps.

The balance between feature learning and collapse in dynamical systems-based generative models

Julian Brandon, Bruno Loureiro, N Alex Cayco-Gajic, Arthur Pellegrino

Over the past decade, a growing body of work has established that implicit biases in learning dynamics fundamentally shape the solutions found by neural networks, governing their learned features and generalisation performance. However, despite these strides in deep learning theory, far less is known about representation learning in generative models based on dynamical systems, from normalising flows to diffusion. Importantly, empirical evidence suggests that continuous-time generative models can be prone to mode collapse, yet a learning theory explaining such biases remains elusive. To address this, we use tools from dynamical systems and operator theory to express the gradient flow of a general class of stochastic differential equations as a composition of linear operators, allowing us to relate collapse to low-rank biases in generative models. In variational inference and flow-matching tasks, we show that this bias can manifest in two different mechanisms: i) where collapsed models are stable fixed points of the gradient flow, or ii) in sequential feature learning, where smaller modes take progressively longer to be learned, producing an effective collapse for a fixed training budget. Together these findings point to a trade-off between feature learning and collapse, suggesting an operator view as a step towards a unified framework for understanding the learning dynamics of dynamical systems-based generative models.

Learning the Brownian Motion in Machine Learning for Parametric Parabolic PDEs: A Backward Quadrature Approach

Qiang Han, Shaolin Ji, Yanan Qi

We propose a backward quadrature learning (BQL) method for solving high dimensional parametric semilinear parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs). To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to extend the applicability of BSDE-based stochastic numerical methods to this class of PDEs. Specifically, inspired by the approach of learning the random variables (LRV)~\cite{BeckerJentzenMullerWurstemberger2024}, BQL incorporates learnable quadrature for Brownian increments at each time level into the backward recursion of BSDEs, enabling more efficient layerwise approximation of local conditional expectations. Furthermore, BQL introduces a continuation surrogate to facilitate the transfer of value-function information from subsequent to preceding time levels, avoiding nested quadrature while controlling the propagation of local approximation errors. Our main theoretical contribution is the derivation of a global error bound that explicitly characterizes the decomposition of the total error, together with a proof of first-order mean-square convergence under suitable approximation and integrability assumptions. Finally, numerical experiments demonstrate that BQL achieves competitive solution accuracy while substantially reducing the amortized computational cost per query. For the HJB benchmark across the tested dimensions, with $Q=7$ parameter queries, BQL achieves computational cost reductions of approximately 40-fold and 4-fold compared with P$^2$INN~[2] and FBSNN~[3], respectively.

György Eigner, Patrik P. Süli, Roland Hollós

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems now produce research results in bulk: in October 2026 a single laboratory released more than 700 AI-generated mathematics manuscripts, only part of them with machine-checked proofs. Such volumes will be checked mostly by AI agents that call specialised software. A passing check certifies only the artefact that was checked. For proofs translated into the proof assistant Lean, it has been shown that this artefact may state something other than the author's text, and that deciding whether a translation is faithful is undecidable in general. We describe two automatic implementations of the tool layer, for mathematics (Wolfram Language, MATLAB, Lean) and statistics (Python and R), and run the mathematical one on a two-claim example. An ambiguous claim was split into two readings, one refuted and one proved.

ProofCoder: Continuous Reasoning for Automated Software Verification

Martim Afonso, Inês Lynce, Maurizio Morisio, João F. Ferreira

Formal verification using proof assistants provides strong correctness guarantees for software, but constructing proofs remains labor-intensive and requires significant expertise. Recent approaches based on large language models (LLMs) have shown promise in automating proof synthesis. However, these approaches typically constrain the model to a rigid tactic-generation loop, in which the surrounding system controls premise retrieval, proof-state management, and backtracking. We introduce ProofCoder, an agentic proof-synthesis system for Rocq that approaches theorem proving from a coding-agent perspective. Rather than generating tactics within a fixed loop, ProofCoder reasons continuously throughout proof search and uses proof-assistant feedback to decide when to gather additional information, inspect proof states, revise previously generated proof fragments, or backtrack to an earlier point in the proof. This allows it to iteratively construct, repair, and refine proofs, at either tactic-level or whole-proof granularity. We evaluate ProofCoder on 280 challenging verification tasks from the AutoRocq Benchmark. On the best-performing configurations, ProofCoder increases proof success from 47% to 60% over the state-of-theart AutoRocq system, corresponding to a 27.7% relative improvement at approximately half the cost. Our ablations identify continuous reasoning as a key design choice: without it, the agent solves fewer lemmas at substantially higher cost, backtracks four to six times more often, and more frequently discards its own revisions. Whole-proof and tactic-level generation achieve similar success rates but follow distinct search and repair strategies. These results suggest that effective automated software verification requires more than improved tactic selection: continuous reasoning and feedback-guided proof revision are critical ingredients for robust proof synthesis.

BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction

Rui Zou, Xin Zhao, Mengqi Wei, Ji-Rong Wen

Large language models (LLMs) have made substantial progress in mathematical reasoning but still produce incorrect solutions to complex problems, indicating the need for more reliable self-correction mechanisms. Mathematical self-correction typically identifies errors through verification, which can proceed backward or forward. The two directions provide complementary verification capabilities. Backward verification checks a candidate answer against the original constraints, bypassing complex intermediate reasoning, but cannot ensure correct reasoning or objective completeness. Forward verification follows the problem conditions and current solution, enabling it to locate reasoning errors and assess objective completeness, but may overlook key constraints in complex solution chains. We propose BiVerify, a dynamically scheduled framework for bidirectional mathematical verification. BiVerify begins with backward verification to screen the answer against the original constraints, followed by forward verification of the reasoning process; subsequent direction switching, state resetting, and re-verification are governed by verification outcomes. Upon failure, the system revises the candidate or verification flow until the same candidate passes both directions. We also introduce shared mathematical tools that separate logical reasoning from precise computation. We systematically evaluate BiVerify on five challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks using multiple mainstream DeepSeek, GPT, and Gemini models. BiVerify exceeds the strongest representative public baseline evaluated in this work by 13.5 percentage points in average accuracy, with the margin reaching 31.2 percentage points on GPT. Further analysis shows that BiVerify's overall performance exceeds that of either verification direction alone.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that our methods deliver competitive performance compared to existing minimax optimization methods.
