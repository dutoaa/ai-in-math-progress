## Executive Summary

September 2026 showed broad, fast-moving progress in AI for mathematics, with especially strong activity around reinforcement learning for reasoning LLMs, physics-informed solvers for PDEs, and automated theorem proving/formalization. Across reasoning, many papers focused on making training more stable, more sample-efficient, and better aligned with credit assignment, retrieval, replay, and self-distillation; several also extended these ideas to multi-agent, multilingual, and safety settings. In PDEs and scientific computing, the month emphasized better optimization geometry, reduced stiffness, discretization-aware formulations, and architectures that handle high dimension, discontinuities, or boundary constraints more robustly. Formal methods and theorem proving continued to mature, with systems that connect informal reasoning to Lean, automate theory construction, and even close longstanding analysis and operator-theory problems. A smaller but important thread concerned mathematical programming as a unifying framework for AI systems, suggesting a growing effort to formalize and optimize the decision-making components of modern AI stacks.

## Major Themes

- RL for reasoning LLMs is becoming more structured, with new work on bilevel optimization, replay control, critic stabilization, policy distillation, and setwise multi-agent updates.
- Credit assignment is a central theme: several papers target token-, step-, trajectory-, and retrieval-level attribution, often using bilevel, clipped, or decoupled objectives.
- Physics-informed learning is shifting from basic PINNs toward curvature-aware optimization, discretization-aware constraints, random features, causal integral architectures, and operator preconditioning.
- High-dimensional PDE solving is increasingly treated as an optimization problem with better geometry, including Gauss-Newton metrics, second-order flow methods, and reduced-space refinement.
- Theorem proving and formalization are moving toward end-to-end agentic systems that generate proofs, construct Lean theories, and verify research-level mathematics.
- Robustness and multilingual behavior remain important: papers examined structural perturbations, tool-use shortcuts, multilingual safety, and trust in teacher signals.
- Mathematical programming is emerging as a common language for AI system design, linking retrieval, routing, alignment, and structured prediction under shared optimization templates.

## Field Progress

- Reasoning-model post-training became more modular and theoretically explicit, with methods targeting staleness, replay, critic noise, objective clipping, and teacher uncertainty rather than treating RL as a black box.
- Multi-agent reasoning matured from generic coordination to principled optimization over sets, graphs, and bilevel structures, better reflecting how collaborative systems actually act.
- PINN research increasingly focused on solver geometry and conditioning, indicating a shift from architecture-only improvements to optimization-aware and discretization-aware design.
- Formalization workflows are now more autonomous and research-capable, with systems that can build intermediate theory, validate assumptions, and integrate informal and formal reasoning loops.
- The field is paying more attention to robustness and failure analysis, including structural sensitivity in multilingual settings, spurious tool-use policies, and unsafe reasoning traces.
- There is growing convergence between optimization theory and AI system design, especially around mathematical programming formulations that unify alignment, routing, retrieval, and control.

## Open Questions

- How can reward signals be decomposed cleanly across policy, retriever, tools, and replay buffers without introducing new optimization pathologies?
- What is the best general framework for scaling PINNs to stiff, discontinuous, or highly heterogeneous PDEs while retaining theoretical guarantees?
- Can formal proof agents be made robust enough to consistently produce novel, research-level mathematics rather than only repackage existing arguments?
- How should multi-agent and multi-step reasoning objectives be structured so that the training unit matches the true causal unit of action?
- Which forms of self-distillation and self-improvement avoid reinforcing teacher errors or hinted-data bias while still improving sample efficiency?
- Can unified mathematical programming abstractions faithfully capture the full range of AI system decisions, or do different subproblems require fundamentally different optimization views?

## Outlook

The month’s work suggests the field is converging on a few durable directions: more principled optimization for reasoning LLMs, more geometry- and structure-aware solvers for scientific computing, and increasingly capable agentic systems for formal mathematics. The strongest papers do not merely add scale; they refine the objective, the action unit, or the computational representation to match the structure of the underlying mathematical problem. If this trend continues, the next phase of AI in math will likely be defined less by generic model improvements and more by carefully engineered learning dynamics, verification loops, and problem-specific optimization frameworks.
